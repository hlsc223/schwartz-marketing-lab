# The Modern Organic Growth Playbook — Phase 2 Argument Map

*How the best B2B brands build durable demand in 2026*

**Two organizing ideas running through every chapter:**
1. Durable demand vs. fragile traffic volume
2. Diagnosis precedes prescription — always

---

## Chapter 1: What happened to B2B traffic

**Core claim:** B2B organic traffic split decisively in 2025–2026, and the split followed a predictable pattern driven by three distinct forces.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That traffic changes in this period were unpredictable, random, or uniformly caused by AI.

**Why this matters:** If you don't understand the forces driving the split, you can't identify which one hit you — and you can't build a strategy to address it.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: Of 118 companies studied, traffic separated into clear cohorts — 44 Grew, 19 Flat, 55 Declined. The distribution wasn't random. It correlated with content type, organic program structure, and category dynamics.
- Finding 2: Three distinct forces operated simultaneously — algorithm updates (Aug 2025 spam update produced a specific multi-bucket long-tail signature), AI Overview SERP interception (satisfiable queries specifically), and AI tool demand substitution (upstream demand for template/procedural content destroyed before the search even happens).
- Finding 3: The dividing variable was content durability. Companies whose core traffic assets were definitional or conceptual held up. Companies whose core assets were tactical, template-based, or actionable-output declined.

**Primary case studies:** Braze (grew via durable content), Aha! (declined via fragile content), Ramp (grew with hidden fragility underneath the headline)

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** PostHog (grew via pure brand demand — different mechanism entirely), ZoomInfo (grew but via a low-quality programmatic pattern — not a replicable blueprint)

**Visual flag:**
- *Traffic distribution chart:* Show the 44/19/55 Grew/Flat/Declined split across 118 companies. Could be a histogram of % traffic change across all companies (x-axis: % change, y-axis: company count) to show the actual distribution and separation. Makes the "decisive split" claim visible at a glance.

**Practical implication:** Before any diagnosis, spend 30 minutes identifying which of the three forces is most active in your category. Check: Has your category seen broad traffic declines (algorithm update likely)? Are your top pages holding position but losing clicks (AIO interception likely)? Has overall search demand for your core query types declined (demand substitution likely)? Each force produces a different signature in your data and requires a different response — getting this wrong at the start will make every subsequent decision wrong too.

**Bridge to next chapter:** Before applying any framework, we need to clear the false priors most people bring into this conversation.

## Chapter 2: The assumptions that didn't survive the data

**Core claim:** The conventional wisdom about what drives B2B organic growth failed in almost every direction the data tested.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That industry consensus accurately reflects what actually works in B2B organic growth in the AI era.

**Why this matters:** Acting on false assumptions leads to misallocated investment. Each assumption this chapter overturns is a specific point where companies are most likely to spend in the wrong direction.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: AI Overviews didn't uniformly destroy informational content. Five independent companies showed definitional content growing throughout the AI Overview expansion window. The suppression was specific to satisfiable queries — not informational content broadly.
- Finding 2: Traffic growth did not reliably reflect business health. LogRocket's organic traffic was 66% below its all-time peak while ARR grew 22% YoY. ZoomInfo's +154% traffic growth was largely driven by a low-quality programmatic pattern. The number and the business moved in different directions.
- Finding 3: No single "hero asset" drove growth in the successful cohort. Across eight growing companies, no single page exceeded ~20% of net gains. Distributed breadth outperformed concentrated bets in every clean-growth case.
- Finding 4: Publishing more content wasn't the answer. Cannibalization rates in the largest content programs (LogRocket 75.6%, Aha! 286.6%) showed that volume alone produces a treadmill — generating more gross gains just to stay even on net.

**Primary case studies:** LogRocket (revenue/traffic divergence), ZoomInfo (traffic quality vs. quantity), Rippling/Navan/Harness (glossary resilience contradicting AIO-kills-informational assumption)

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** GitHub (large, authoritative site still declined — scale doesn't protect you)

**Visual flag:**
- *Assumptions vs. reality table:* Two-column table. Left column: "What the industry assumed." Right column: "What the data showed." Four to five rows covering the key overturned assumptions above. Clean, readable, highly shareable.

**Practical implication:** Take your current content roadmap and run it against the four overturned assumptions. For each planned investment, ask: Am I doing this because I believe AI Overviews suppress informational content broadly? Because I assume traffic growth means program health? Because I'm betting on a hero asset? Because I think publishing more will compound? If the answer to any of those is yes, you've found a specific misalignment worth examining before committing further resources.

**Bridge to next chapter:** Now that the false priors are cleared, we can look at real traffic data with fresh eyes. But first: your headline number is probably misleading you.

## Chapter 3: Why you can't trust your traffic number

**Core claim:** In the majority of cases studied, headline traffic figures misrepresented the underlying health of the organic program.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That total organic traffic is a reliable proxy for organic program health.

**Why this matters:** Diagnosis built on an unverified number leads to the wrong treatment. Decomposition is the prerequisite — not an optional step.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: Branded/non-branded decomposition revealed hidden risk in "growing" companies and hidden strength in "declining" ones. Rippling's brand traffic was driving the headline while non-branded content was churning. CircleCI's recovery showed up in non-branded before it showed in the headline number.
- Finding 2: Traffic value divergence from raw traffic was consistently the first signal of a quality or composition problem. ZoomInfo: traffic +154.7% vs. traffic value +14.7% — a 140-point gap. GitHub: traffic value fell half as fast as raw traffic during decline, signaling authority retention despite volume loss.
- Finding 3: Category baseline comparison changed the interpretation in every case it was applied. Freshworks's decline looked catastrophic in isolation; it looked less anomalous against a category with genuine headwind. Braze's growth was impressive; it looked even stronger against a majority-declining category.

**Primary case studies:** ZoomInfo (TV/traffic divergence), GitHub (TV divergence in decline — authority retained), Gainsight (single branded keyword explained 65% of apparent "growth")

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** Braze (decomposition confirmed the headline story rather than overturning it — demonstrates decomposition isn't always alarming, just necessary)

**Visual flag:**
- *Traffic value divergence illustration:* A simple 2x2 matrix: traffic direction (up/down) on one axis, traffic value direction (up/down) on the other. Each quadrant labeled with what it means diagnostically (e.g., traffic up + value flat = quality problem; traffic down + value holds = authority retained despite volume loss).

**Practical implication:** Before any strategic decision, spend one hour running three checks: (1) split your traffic into branded vs. non-branded — if branded accounts for more than 40% of your total, your headline number is misleading you; (2) compare your traffic value trend to your raw traffic trend — a gap of 20+ points signals a quality or composition problem; (3) check your category baseline against a comparable competitor. If they declined similarly, the problem is market-wide, not strategic. Do all three before drawing any conclusions.

**Bridge to next chapter:** Once you have a verified picture of your traffic, the next question is: which specific mechanism caused what you're seeing?

## Chapter 4: Which mechanism hit you

**Core claim:** Traffic declines have five distinct causes, each producing a different data signature and requiring a different response.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That declining traffic is a single, undifferentiated problem with a single solution.

**Why this matters:** Misdiagnosing the mechanism leads to investing in the wrong fix. Recovery investment applied to a structural problem produces cost, not recovery. This is the chapter that makes diagnosis actionable.

**Opening context (from Ch 3 decomposition output):** Once you have a verified picture of your traffic, you can identify which trajectory you're in. Across the cohort, five distinct trajectories appeared: Continuous Acceleration, Steady Growth, Growth then Pullback, Decline then Recovery, Steady Decline. Each has a different underlying mechanism — and the same trajectory can be caused by different mechanisms. The visual taxonomy (five trajectory types, one curve each) is the starting point; the evidence chain below is how you identify which mechanism produced the trajectory you're looking at.

**Visual flag (moved from Ch 3):**
- *Five trajectory types gallery:* Small schematic traffic curves for each of the five trajectory types with a one-line label beneath each. Use as visual context-setter at the top of the chapter before the decision tree — makes "which trajectory are you in?" concrete before "which mechanism caused it?" becomes the question.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: Algorithm update damage — specifically spam updates — produces a distinct signature: sudden, simultaneous multi-bucket long-tail collapse (positions 11-20, 21-50, 51+ all dropping in the same short window). CloudBees and Ramp both showed this pattern, both aligned to the Aug 2025 spam update. Distinct from competitive displacement (gradual position erosion over months) and AIO interception (position unchanged, traffic drops).
- Finding 2: AI Overview interception has a unique, directly observable signature — same-position CTR collapse. When a page holds or improves its ranking and simultaneously loses 80–99% of traffic, the mechanism is SERP interception. Observed across 20+ keywords for Aha!, including pages that moved from position 3 to position 1 and still lost 92% of traffic.
- Finding 3: Architectural damage produces a specific timing pattern — sharp, sudden decline aligned to a dateable event not correlated with any algorithm update. Freshworks: the "Dew design system" launch (Dec 1, 2025) produced multi-bucket ranking loss with preserved backlink equity. The backlinks stayed; the rankings didn't.
- Finding 4: AI tool demand substitution is upstream of the SERP. Search volume itself declines — not just CTR or ranking. Template content (-60.3% at Aha!), developer tutorials for mature libraries (LogRocket), and procedural how-to content are the highest-exposure types. Refreshing content doesn't restore demand that has migrated to AI generation.

**Primary case studies:** CloudBees (algorithm update signature), Aha! (AIO interception + demand substitution), Freshworks (architectural damage), LogRocket (demand substitution for developer tutorials)

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** GitHub (AI mechanism plausible but couldn't be cleanly isolated — large sites may face compound mechanisms simultaneously, making clean attribution difficult)

**Visual flag:**
- *Diagnostic decision tree:* A branching flowchart. Starting questions: "Is the decline sudden or gradual?" → "Is it correlated with a known dated event (redesign, algorithm update)?" → "Does position data show same-position traffic collapse or actual ranking drops?" → "Is it concentrated in specific position buckets (11-20, 21-50, 51+) or spread across all?" → "Has search volume for the affected queries declined?" Each branch leads to a mechanism label and brief description. This is the chapter's flagship visual and one of the report's most shareable assets.

**Practical implication:** Before committing to any recovery investment, run the decision tree — takes 2–3 hours of data review. The questions are sequential: Is the decline sudden (days/weeks) or gradual (months)? Does the timing align to a specific event — redesign, update, product launch? Does position data show ranking drops or same-position CTR collapse? Is the collapse concentrated in positions 21-50 and 51+ specifically? Has search volume for the affected queries declined? Each answer eliminates mechanisms. Identify the mechanism before spending anything on recovery — the wrong treatment wastes both the money and the window when recovery momentum matters most.

**Bridge to next chapter:** Now that we've covered decline and diagnosis, let's look at what the growing companies were actually doing differently.

## Chapter 5: The anatomy of durable growth

**Core claim:** Durable growth was consistently associated with broad, distributed content programs and definitional content — not with any single tactic, asset, or concentration strategy.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That growth comes from finding the one thing that works and scaling it.

**Why this matters:** Concentrated content bets are fragile. A single page or cluster can lose its ranking for any number of reasons. Distributed breadth compounds; concentration concentrates risk into a single point of failure.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: Across eight growing companies, no single page exceeded ~20% of net traffic gains. Braze, Navan, Harness, Vanta, and Ramp all showed this distribution pattern independently. The breadth was consistent across different categories, content approaches, and company sizes.
- Finding 2: Definitional ("what is X") content proved consistently durable across categories — HR (Rippling), Finance (Navan, Ramp), DevOps (Harness), Security (Vanta, Hyperproof). Five companies independently confirmed this pattern across the AI Overview expansion window. The industry prediction that AI Overviews would suppress this content type did not materialize.
- Finding 3: Position consolidation — positions 1-3 and 4-10 growing while 21-50 and 51+ collapse — appeared in seven of the growing companies as a leading health signal. A shrinking long-tail alongside a growing top-position footprint is not a warning sign; it is a sign that quality is concentrating at the top of the SERP.
- Finding 4: Ranking breakthroughs on existing pages with no detectable new backlinks occurred in at least two cases (Akita, CloudBees). Growth can come from re-ranking, not just new content — an important implication for programs evaluating whether to create or optimize.

**Primary case studies:** Braze (cleanest broad content growth case), Harness (DevOps definitional content), Ramp (glossary + free tools at scale), Vanta (product launch + broad compliance content)

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** PostHog (grew +131% but via pure brand demand — no content mechanism; confirms the pattern is a dominant tendency, not a universal law), Akita (single-page concentration as the exception that proves the rule — and even then, two pages, not one, drove the breakout)

**Visual flags:**
- *Content distribution chart:* For 2-3 growing companies, show the top 10 pages' share of net traffic gain as a bar or column chart. Visually demonstrates the "no dominant page" pattern — the bars are roughly similar in height, not dominated by one outlier.
- *Content durability matrix:* Content types on the vertical axis (definitional/what-is, methodology, strategy/marketing, template, role/career, how-to procedural, free tool). Durability evidence on the horizontal axis (strong evidence of durability → strong evidence of fragility). Each cell populated with a finding or example. One of the report's flagship reference visuals.

**Practical implication:** This week, pull your Top Pages report and calculate two numbers: (1) What percentage of your net organic traffic gains over the past 12 months came from your single top-performing page? If it's above 20%, you have concentration risk. (2) What percentage came from your top 5 pages combined? If it's above 60%, your program is fragile. Identify two or three content areas adjacent to your strongest performers where you have no significant presence — those are your first diversification targets.

**Bridge to next chapter:** But growth doesn't tell the whole story. Some of the growing companies in this study were sitting on hidden fragility that their headline numbers completely obscured.

## Chapter 6: Hidden risk in growing companies

**Core claim:** A growing headline traffic number can mask severe underlying fragility — high cannibalization, long-tail collapse, and brand-dependency that make organic programs unsustainable.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That growing traffic means a healthy organic program.

**Why this matters (if this chapter disappeared):** Readers would wrongly assume that a positive headline number means their organic strategy is sound — and would miss the warning signals until they become a decline.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: Cannibalization rate as a portfolio health metric. Gross gain vs. gross loss tells a fundamentally different story from net gain alone. LogRocket: 75.6% cannibalization (for every 1 unit of traffic gained, 0.76 was simultaneously lost on other pages). Aha!: 286.6% (2.87 units lost per unit gained). At high cannibalization rates, a content program must generate 3–4x its desired net gain in gross gains just to stay even — a treadmill that accelerates as the portfolio grows.
- Finding 2: Long-tail position collapse invisible in the headline number. Ramp grew substantially in headline traffic while simultaneously absorbing a severe collapse in positions 21-50 and 51+ aligned to the Aug 2025 spam update. The top-position gains masked it entirely in the net number. Readers who looked only at the headline would see a healthy growing company. The position data told a different story.
- Finding 3: Brand growth masking content decay. Several "Grew" companies' headline growth was primarily driven by branded or homepage traffic with little underlying non-branded content program growth. Brand momentum is not a content strategy — when it slows, there is nothing underneath to sustain the number.

**Primary case studies:** Ramp (position collapse masked by headline growth), LogRocket (high cannibalization rate in developer blog), CloudBees (brand/content divergence — brand traffic declined while non-branded grew, opposite directions)

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** Braze, Navan (grew with genuinely clean signals — low cannibalization, broad distribution, no masked collapse — demonstrates that clean growth is possible and distinguishable)

**Visual flag:**
- *Cannibalization rate waterfall chart:* For 3-4 companies, show gross gain (positive bar), gross loss (negative bar), and net (resulting bar). Makes the "treadmill" concept immediately visible. Companies: clean-growth case (Braze ~10% cannibalization), moderate case (Ramp), high case (LogRocket 75.6%), extreme case (Aha! 286.6%). The visual contrast tells the story faster than prose.

**Practical implication:** Pull a Top Pages export for two periods — now vs. 12 months ago. Calculate: gross gain (sum of traffic increases across all pages), gross loss (sum of traffic decreases), net (gross gain minus gross loss). Divide gross loss by gross gain — that's your cannibalization rate. Under 30%: healthy. 30–60%: moderate, worth monitoring. Over 60%: your program is on a treadmill — generating significantly more gross gain than net gain. Over 100%: you're net negative; publishing more content at current quality is accelerating the problem, not solving it.

**Bridge to next chapter:** Understanding fragility in growing companies prepares the ground for the recovery question: when a decline actually hits, what does it take to come back?

## Chapter 7: How companies actually recovered

**Core claim:** Recovery followed three archetypes — and the failure mechanism determined which archetype applied.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That recovery requires publishing new content to replace what was lost.

**Why this matters (if this chapter disappeared):** Readers would approach recovery without a map — potentially pursuing the wrong archetype for their failure mechanism and wasting the period when recovery momentum matters most.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: Where losses were concentrated was the single most predictive variable for whether recovery was possible — and which path it took. Companies whose losses fell on peripheral/topical content recovered. Companies whose losses fell on foundational pages did not (within the study window). This pattern held across categories.
- Finding 2: The three recovery archetypes, with case evidence:
  - *Content cluster breakthrough (CircleCI):* A definitional/comparison content cluster emerged as the recovery driver. Pages already existed; rankings improved without new investment. The cluster answered "what is X vs. Y" queries in a category where people were actively seeking this education.
  - *Top-position momentum as self-executing recovery (Hyperproof):* Top-position content never faltered during the mid/long-tail collapse. Recovery came from that existing momentum extending and a ranking breakthrough on one high-volume query ("risk management frameworks," 56k volume, pos 21 → pos 8). The recovery was largely self-executing because the foundation never broke.
  - *Ranking breakthrough on existing pages with no new links (CloudBees):* A single existing git-tutorial blog post moved from obscurity to high rankings with no detectable new backlink investment. Recovery came from a re-ranking event, not new content.
- Boundary condition (LogRocket): Headline traffic recovered through branded geographic expansion — brand keyword demand expanded to new country variants (191% growth on "logrocket" keyword across country expansions). Recovery in the headline number was primarily branded, not content-driven. This mechanism operates independently of content program architecture and does not require a surviving structural floor. Not a formal archetype.
- Finding 3: Recovery timelines varied from 2-4 months (Hyperproof's top-position momentum provided a fast floor) to 6+ months. The fastest recoveries involved top-position content that never stopped performing during the decline.

**Primary case studies:** CircleCI (content cluster), Hyperproof (top-position self-executing recovery), CloudBees (ranking breakthrough on existing page)

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** Freshworks, Secureframe, Sprinto, Userpilot — no recovery within window. Each illustrates a structural failure where no viable recovery archetype was available: foundational pages lost, architectural damage sustained, or update-cadence vulnerability without a top-position foundation to recover from. LogRocket — headline recovery through branded geographic expansion; brand demand expansion operates independently of content program architecture and is not a structural archetype.

**Visual flag:**
- *Recovery archetypes diagram:* Three-panel visual, one panel per archetype. Each panel shows: archetype name, the traffic curve shape (schematic line chart showing trough and recovery), the key condition required for this archetype to apply, and the primary case study company. Makes the three paths visually distinct and memorable.

**Practical implication:** Before spending on recovery content, run the archetype matching test in sequence: (1) Did your losses concentrate on peripheral/topical pages, or on foundational pages? (2) Did your top-ranking pages (positions 1–3) hold position during the decline, or did they also fall? (3) Do you have an existing content cluster with multiple mid-ranking pages that could consolidate? Your answers map to which archetype (if any) applies. If foundational pages collapsed and your top positions fell, no archetype in this study produced recovery within 18 months — redirect that investment instead of pursuing recovery.

**Bridge to next chapter:** But not every decline leads to a recovery opportunity. Sometimes the right strategy isn't recovery at all.

## Chapter 8: When recovery isn't the right strategy

**Core claim:** Some declines are structural — and applying recovery investment to structural problems produces cost without recovery.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That all traffic declines can be reversed with the right SEO investment.

**Why this matters (if this chapter disappeared):** Readers would waste investment trying to recover structural losses using tactical SEO — exactly the wrong prescription for the diagnosis.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: Pillar content collapse — when the pages that define a site's core topical authority lose their rankings — does not recover through content refreshes or link building. Secureframe and Sprinto both lost their foundational compliance-framework pages ("what is SOC 2," "what is ISO 27001"). These aren't peripheral content; they are the reason the site existed organically. No recovery within the 18-month study window. By contrast, Hyperproof lost peripheral content (incident response, FedRAMP, DevSecOps) while core GRC pages held — and recovered to a new all-time high.
- Finding 2: Architectural damage from redesigns. On-page content changes can break existing rankings even when URLs and backlinks are preserved. Freshworks: ranking loss aligned precisely to the Dew design system launch (Dec 1, 2025). Backlink equity was retained; rankings weren't. Publishing more content doesn't repair a ranking signal broken when on-page targeting changed.
- Finding 3: Upstream demand substitution — when search volume itself declines because AI tools are substituting for the query. Aha!'s template content lost 60.3% of traffic across 154 pages. This isn't a ranking problem. The searches that previously happened are no longer happening at the same volume. Refreshing templates won't restore demand that has moved to AI generation.

**Primary case studies:** Freshworks (architectural damage), Secureframe/Sprinto (pillar collapse — direct contrast with Hyperproof), Aha! (demand substitution for template content), Userpilot (update-cadence vulnerability with no structural lever)

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** CloudBees (appeared structural initially due to severe long-tail collapse — but was algorithm-update driven and partially recoverable because top-position content never stopped performing. Demonstrates the importance of correctly diagnosing the mechanism before concluding it's structural.)

**Visual flag:**
- *Structural vs. tactical decision matrix:* A table or 2x2. Rows: type of decline (pillar content loss / architectural damage / demand substitution / algorithm update / competitive displacement). Columns: evidence signals to look for + recommended response (recover vs. redirect investment). Gives readers a quick-reference diagnostic tool they can use immediately.

**Practical implication:** Before any recovery spend, run your declined pages through the structural vs. tactical matrix. If two or more structural signals are present — pillar pages lost, redesign-aligned timing, search volume decline — stop recovery investment on those assets immediately. Redirect the question: what does this audience still need that AI tools and current SERP behavior haven't already satisfied? That's what to build. If you can't identify a clear answer, the traffic may be permanently gone and the investment belongs elsewhere.

**Bridge to next chapter:** Understanding where AI fits changes both the diagnosis and the forward-looking strategy — and it's more nuanced than most people expect.

## Chapter 9: What AI Overviews actually do to traffic

**Core claim:** AI Overviews suppress traffic specifically on satisfiable queries — where the SERP can complete the user's task without a click — and leave engagement-requiring content largely intact.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That AI Overviews are either uniformly bad for search traffic or uniformly neutral — the nuance is in the query type, not the content category.

**Why this matters:** Without knowing which pages sit on satisfiable queries, you can't predict or prevent interception. The exposure is measurable and auditable — it is not random.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: Five companies independently showed definitional content growing or holding throughout the AI Overview expansion window — Rippling, Navan, Harness, Vanta, and Ramp. This directly contradicts the industry prediction that informational content would be broadly suppressed. The resistance was consistent across HR, Finance, DevOps, Security, and FinTech categories.
- Finding 2: Same-position CTR collapse — the most direct AI Overview interception evidence in this study. Aha!'s keyword data shows 20+ queries where position held or improved while traffic fell 79–99%. Specific examples: "user story template" moved position 3 → 1 (improved) with traffic dropping 108 → 9 (−92%). When ranking improves and traffic collapses, the mechanism is SERP interception, not competitive displacement.
- Finding 3: The distinguishing variable is query satisfiability. Role lists ("marketing job titles" — AI can enumerate the list), templates ("user story template" — AI can provide the artifact), brief definitions ("product marketing" — AI can define it in the Overview), step-by-step processes ("product positioning") all saw severe CTR collapse. Content requiring continued engagement — comprehensive methodology guides, conceptual frameworks, proprietary research — proved resistant.
- Finding 4: Complete query routing as an extreme case. Freshworks's "is this website down" query routed entirely to specialist uptime tools across all six AI engines tested. The AI system didn't attempt to answer from content — it delegated to a tool. This represents the extreme end of satisfiable-query routing and is a distinct mechanism from standard AIO interception.

**Primary case studies:** Aha! (primary same-position CTR collapse evidence — most direct in the study), Rippling/Navan/Harness/Vanta/Ramp (resistance confirmation across five independent companies), Freshworks (complete query routing)

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** GitHub (declined but AI mechanism couldn't be cleanly isolated from other forces — demonstrates that even authority and scale don't guarantee protection on the most satisfiable query types)

**Visual flags:**
- *Same-position CTR collapse table:* A table showing 8-10 keywords from Aha!'s keyword export. Columns: keyword, search volume, previous position, current position, previous traffic, current traffic, traffic change %. Sorted by traffic drop. Makes the pattern undeniable in a single visual — position held or improved while traffic vanished.
- *Query satisfiability spectrum:* A horizontal spectrum from "fully satisfiable in SERP" (left) to "requires continued engagement" (right). Populated with content type examples at each point on the spectrum: role lists and templates on the left (high AIO risk), comprehensive methodology guides and original research on the right (low AIO risk), with middle cases in between. One of the report's most immediately usable reference visuals.

**Practical implication:** This week, take your top 20 traffic pages and test each one: search the page's primary keyword in an incognito window and check for an AI Overview. If one appears, ask: can the user accomplish their goal from the Overview alone, or do they need to click through? If they don't need to click — list, template, brief definition, step-by-step process — that page is AIO exposure. Any page in your top 10 by traffic volume with confirmed AIO exposure is worth either deepening (add proprietary data, methodology, or a dimension the AI can't synthesize) or deprioritizing for future investment.

**Bridge to next chapter:** AI Overviews are one part of the AI picture. There is also the question of how AI systems — chatbots, answer engines, AI search — find and cite content when users don't go to Google at all.

## Chapter 10: Being found by AI systems

**Core claim:** AI systems tend to reward the same content properties that made search content durable — but retrieval depends on query type, competing authorities, and engine behavior, and these conditions create important exceptions.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That AI discoverability and search discoverability are either completely identical or completely separate strategies.

**Why this matters:** Building two separate content programs — one for search, one for AI — is inefficient and probably unnecessary. But assuming they're identical leads to gaps where AI systems won't retrieve your content even when search does.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: Substantial overlap between search-performing and AI-cited content. Of 13 growth-asset prompts run across six AI engines, target company domains were cited in 9 cases. In 7 of those 9, the exact target URL was confirmed. Blog and educational articles dominated citations (5 of 9 confirmed cases). The content properties that drive both are consistent: specific, authoritative, directly answering a question with genuine information gain.
- Finding 2: Important exceptions driven by query type — not content quality. Operational queries route to specialist tools regardless of content quality (Freshworks's uptime query cited only dedicated uptime monitors across all six engines). Institutional authority queries route to regulatory or governance bodies (Vanta's "risk management frameworks" — Vanta's largest traffic-gaining page — was not cited on any engine; NIST, AWS, and governance authorities dominated). These are structural routing decisions, not content quality failures.
- Finding 3: Engine differences are real and material. Google AI Mode and AI Overviews showed higher citation rates for cohort content than ChatGPT and Copilot in this study. Perplexity cited across a broader range of sources. AI discoverability is not uniform — the engine matters, and different audiences use different engines.
- Finding 4: The citation research confirms the content durability findings from search. Educational, authoritative, directly-answering content with genuine information gain is most frequently retrieved — the same type that proved most durable in organic search.

**Primary case studies:** CircleCI (blog post cited on 4 of 6 engines — strongest citation case in the cohort, directly corresponding to its recovery driver), Rippling (glossary page cited — confirms AI retrievability of the same content type that showed AIO resistance), Vanta (not cited on risk frameworks despite it being the largest traffic-gaining page — illustrates institutional authority displacement)

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** Freshworks and Gainsight (non-cited due to query type routing, not content quality — Freshworks routes to uptime tools, Gainsight routes to NPS-specialist tools. Demonstrates that content can be excellent and still not be cited if the query type structurally routes elsewhere.)

**Visual flag:**
- *AI citation matrix:* Grid showing cohort companies on the vertical axis and six AI engines (ChatGPT, Copilot, Google AI Mode, Google AI Overviews, Google Gemini, Perplexity) on the horizontal axis. Cells: confirmed citation (filled), partial/secondary URL (half-filled), not cited (empty). Makes the engine variation and coverage gaps visible at a glance. One of the study's most original data assets.

**Practical implication:** Pick your three most important category queries — the ones you most want to be found for in AI systems. Run each through ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Mode. Note: (1) whether your domain is cited; (2) whether a competitor is cited instead; (3) whether the response routes to a specialist tool or institutional authority. If you're not cited but a competitor is, the gap is almost always content depth or specificity on that exact question — that's fixable. If the query routes structurally away from all brands (uptime tools, regulatory bodies), content investment won't change that outcome — stop measuring AI citation on those queries and invest accordingly.

**Bridge to next chapter:** Having understood what happened, how to diagnose it, what the AI era actually means — we can now build a complete framework for what to do next.

## Chapter 11: The durable demand playbook

**Core claim:** Durable demand requires a different philosophy of content investment — optimizing for content that creates ongoing engagement and compounds over time, rather than content that satisfies a one-time query and loses the user.

**Assumption this chapter challenges:** That organic content strategy is primarily a question of what topics to cover and how many pieces to produce.

**Why this matters (if this chapter disappeared):** Readers would leave the report knowing what went wrong but without a clear framework for what to build instead. The research would have diagnosed without prescribing.

**Evidence chain:**
- Finding 1: Content durability tiers — confirmed from cross-cohort evidence across 18+ companies. Definitional/conceptual content: highest durability. Broad educational content: durable. Proprietary methodology/original research: durable. Tactical how-to for mature processes: fragile. Template/actionable-output: fragile. Role/list/career: fragile. These tiers are observable, measurable, and consistent across categories and company sizes.
- Finding 2: Portfolio construction signals from winning companies. Low cannibalization rate (gross loss <30% of gross gain). Non-branded traffic as the primary growth driver. Position consolidation toward top buckets as a health signal. No single page above ~20% of net gains. These are not strategic principles — they are measurable, observable portfolio health metrics.
- Finding 3: Investment reallocation implications from the evidence. Stop: template libraries, role/career lists, basic how-to content for mature/well-documented processes. De-risk: any content whose primary value is satisfying a one-time SERP query. Double down on: definitional depth, proprietary methodology, original data, entity-rich conceptual frameworks, free tools that require ongoing engagement.
- Finding 4: The two-question test for any new content investment. (1) Does this create ongoing engagement — will the user return, share, cite, or deepen their relationship with it over time? (2) Can an AI system fully satisfy the query that would bring them here without a click? If the answer to (2) is yes, the investment is structurally fragile — regardless of execution quality.

**Primary case studies:** Ramp (most complete execution of durable demand principles at scale — glossary + free tools + minimal cannibalization), Braze (clean early example of broad educational content compounding), Harness (definitional content durable in a highly technical DevOps category), CircleCI (recovery via durable content cluster — shows the playbook working in a recovery context, not just a growth context)

**Counterexamples / boundary conditions:** Aha! (high content investment, high category authority, still declined — because the investment was concentrated in fragile content types. The clearest illustration that philosophy matters more than volume or effort.)

**Visual flags:**
- *Content durability matrix (full version):* The flagship reference visual for the entire report. Content types on the vertical axis. Three columns: (1) AIO vulnerability (high/medium/low based on satisfiability), (2) AI citation likelihood (high/medium/low based on Scrunch data), (3) historical durability in this study (strong evidence / mixed / fragile). Aha!'s and Ramp's content portfolios could be overlaid as examples of "fragile mix" vs. "durable mix."
- *Portfolio health scorecard:* A table of five metrics — cannibalization rate, branded/non-branded traffic ratio, position distribution (% in 1-10 vs. 21+), content-type mix (% definitional vs. template), single-page concentration (top page as % of net gains) — with benchmark ranges from the study (e.g., cannibalization rate: healthy <30%, moderate 30-60%, concerning >60%). Readers can fill in their own numbers.

**Practical implication:** Block two hours this week and run the full audit. (1) Take your top 50 pages by traffic and classify each against the content durability matrix — mark each as durable, mixed, or fragile. (2) Calculate your cannibalization rate from a 12-month Top Pages export. (3) Run your top 10 pages through the AIO satisfiability test. (4) For every item on your current content roadmap, apply the two-question test: does this create ongoing engagement, and can an AI fully satisfy the query without a click? At the end, you have four outputs: a stop list, a de-risk list, a double-down list, and a clear decision framework for every content investment going forward. That's the entire report, applied.
