# Chapter 1: What Happened to B2B Traffic

---

B2B organic traffic split decisively in 2025 and 2026. Not uniformly — in opposite directions simultaneously, across companies in the same categories, using the same strategies, targeting the same types of queries. Some programs grew by double digits. Some lost half their traffic. Some lost more.

The split wasn't random. It did not map cleanly to company size, domain authority, publishing volume, or backlink growth. It followed a pattern — and the pattern is the subject of this report.

This study tracked 118 B2B SaaS companies across an 18-month window, examining their organic traffic, position data, traffic value, and content composition in enough detail to ask why programs with similar resources and intentions ended up in such different places. The answer that emerged from that data isn't the one most people in organic growth would have predicted.

The strongest recurring differentiator wasn't effort, scale, or technical sophistication. It was what kind of content the program had built.

---

## The split

Of the 118 companies tracked, 44 grew organic traffic over the study window, 19 stayed roughly flat, and 55 declined. That distribution — 37% growing, 16% flat, 47% declining — understates the divergence. The companies that grew often grew substantially. The companies that declined often lost a significant share of what they had built over years.

More striking than the distribution is what it correlated with. The cohort didn't divide cleanly along industry lines, company stage, or budget. Companies in the same category, competing for the same queries, landed on opposite sides of the split. The difference was not where they competed but what they had built to compete there.

## Three forces, not one

The split was produced by three distinct forces operating simultaneously across the cohort. Understanding them as three separate mechanisms — rather than as a single undifferentiated "AI disruption" — is what makes diagnosis and response possible.

**Algorithm updates.** The August 2025 spam update produced the study's most identifiable algorithm-driven signature: sudden, simultaneous collapse across positions 11–20, 21–50, and 51+, within a narrow time window aligned to the update date. Companies whose programs depended on mid-tier rankings in vulnerable content categories absorbed this loss acutely. Companies whose programs were anchored in top positions on high-authority pages held or recovered.

**AI Overview interception.** At the SERP level, AI Overviews began intercepting clicks on specific types of queries — not informational content broadly, but queries that could be fully satisfied in the Overview response itself. The signature was unusual: rankings held or improved while traffic fell 80–99% on the same pages. Aha! provided the clearest evidence in the cohort, with 20+ queries where pages held or improved their rankings while traffic fell 80–99%.

**AI tool demand substitution.** Furthest upstream, search demand for entire query types declined — not because AI Overviews answered those queries in the SERP, but because users were generating the answers directly from AI tools before ever searching. Template content, procedural how-to content for well-documented processes, and developer tutorials for mature technologies all showed this pattern: declining search volume on the affected queries, not just declining click-through rates. The search that would have led to the click was no longer happening.

> **The three forces were simultaneous, but they were not the same. They produced different signatures in the data, required different diagnoses, and demanded different responses. Programs that treated them as a single problem called "AI" applied the same response to mechanisms that had nothing in common.**

These three forces explain most of the environment-level pressure in the cohort, but they were not the only diagnosable causes of decline. Chapter 4 expands the picture into five mechanisms by adding two patterns that repeatedly surfaced in the data but were not produced by the primary AI forces: architectural damage and competitive displacement.

---

## What determined which side of the split a program landed on

Content type was the most consistent predictor.

A classification pass across all 44 growing companies — combining Phase 3 deep-dive evidence with web research and Ahrefs Top Pages analysis — found that definitional and educational content was the most common primary growth pattern among full-weight classifiable growers (23 of 38 full-weight classifiable growers, 61%), ahead of brand-led growth (7), mixed growth patterns (7), and programmatic content (1). That does not make DE the only path to growing organic traffic, but it shows the cases examined in depth were not isolated exceptions. The classification pass does not independently establish causality — it broadens the evidence base and characterizes the cohort; the causal argument rests on the mechanism-level evidence in the deep dives. The boundary conditions at the end of this chapter describe where the evidence points in a different direction.

The programs that grew through content compounding had one thing in common that cut across category, company size, and content volume: their core traffic assets were content types that required continued engagement to be useful. Definitional and conceptual content. Comprehensive methodology guides. Proprietary frameworks and original research. Tools and resources that required the reader to interact rather than extract.

These content types consistently showed the greatest resilience across all three forces. They weren't satisfiable in an AI Overview response, because the value required a click. They weren't displaced by AI tool generation, because the value wasn't a one-time output. They weren't uniquely vulnerable to algorithm updates, because the authority signals underlying them were broad and distributed.

The programs that declined, despite many being well executed, concentrated their traffic in content types with the opposite property: content whose primary value was providing a direct answer, a template, a list, or a step-by-step process that a user could extract and use without engaging further. These content types were precisely what the three forces affected most. Whether the underlying query could be satisfied in the SERP without a click — a distinction the report formalizes as *query satisfiability* in Chapter 9 — turned out to be the most consistent structural predictor of AIO exposure across the cohort.

This is the difference between *durable demand* and *fragile traffic*. Durable demand is built on content that compounds — that creates ongoing engagement, develops relationships with readers, and renews itself because the audience that needs it keeps arriving. Fragile traffic is built on content that satisfies — that provides a one-time answer and loses the user the moment they have it.

---

## What the cases showed

**Braze** is the clearest example of a program built on durable demand. Traffic grew broadly and consistently throughout the study window — not from a single breakout asset, but from a wide distribution of educational and conceptual content serving a marketing practitioner audience with genuine ongoing learning needs. The program wasn't immune to the environment; it was built in a way that the environment's primary forces didn't structurally threaten.

**Aha!** is the clearest example of a program built on fragile traffic. One of the most mature content programs in the cohort — extensive investment, genuine category authority, well-executed pages. It still declined substantially, because the investment was concentrated in template and tactical output content — the exact types most exposed to AI Overview interception and, in some cases, AI tool demand substitution upstream. Execution quality and effort didn't offset a structural mismatch between content type and durability.

**Ramp** sits in a more instructive position: classified as a growing company by headline traffic, and growing by that measure, but with hidden fragility that the headline obscured. Ramp's program included both durable assets — a financial glossary, free tools requiring genuine engagement — and vulnerability to the August 2025 spam update that damaged mid-tier and long-tail rankings. The headline growth was real. The underlying composition was more fragile than the number suggested.

---

## Boundary conditions

**PostHog (+131% traffic growth):** PostHog grew substantially during the study window, but the mechanism was primarily brand demand rather than content compounding. Developer community growth drove navigational search volume — people searching for PostHog by name at an increasing rate. That's a genuine organic signal and a meaningful business outcome, but it's not a content strategy result. It demonstrates that traffic growth and content program compounding can come apart: PostHog grew because more people knew what it was, not because the content program was building new organic demand.

**ZoomInfo (+154.7% traffic growth):** ZoomInfo's headline growth was the highest in the cohort. Its traffic value growth was 14.7% over the same period — a 140-point divergence. The composition of ZoomInfo's traffic shifted dramatically toward low-intent, low-value queries during the growth period, likely driven by a programmatic content pattern. It represents the edge case where traffic growth can be a misleading signal in the positive direction: a large number built on a composition that doesn't reflect durable demand.

> **Research synthesis**
>
> *B2B organic traffic split decisively in 2025–2026, with 44 of 118 companies growing and 55 declining. Among content-driven growing companies, the split correlated with content type — specifically whether a program's core assets created ongoing engagement or satisfied a one-time query. Brand-led growth operated through a separate mechanism, documented in the boundary conditions. Three forces drove the environment: algorithm updates, AI Overview SERP interception, and upstream AI tool demand substitution. Each produced a distinct data signature and required a distinct response.*

## What one sentence should you remember from this chapter?

B2B organic traffic didn't decline uniformly in 2025–2026 — it split, along a line defined by content durability, driven by three forces that most programs weren't diagnosing separately.

---

## Before you move on

Before applying any diagnostic framework, spend 30 minutes identifying which of the three forces is most active in your category and program. The question matters because each force requires a different response — and treating them as a single problem produces a single response that fits none of them well.

| Force | Signal to look for in your data |
|---|---|
| Algorithm update damage | Broad category decline? Does your traffic drop align to a known update date? Do position losses concentrate in the 11–50 range simultaneously? |
| AI Overview interception | Are your highest-ranking pages losing clicks without losing positions? Do affected queries return an AI Overview that fully answers the user's task? |
| AI tool demand substitution | Is search volume itself declining on your affected queries — not just CTR or rankings? Are the affected pages primarily template, procedural, or tutorial content? |

Identify the force before investing in a response. The next chapter clears several assumptions that make this identification harder than it needs to be.
