# Chapter 11: The Durable Demand Playbook

---

Every chapter in this report has been a diagnosis. This one is the prescription.

Across 118 companies, 15 deep dives, a classification pass across all 44 growing companies, and 18 months of traffic behavior, the same investment question kept reappearing in different forms: is this content type building durable demand, or is it borrowing fragile traffic from a system that no longer rewards it? This chapter turns those findings into a single decision framework.

What remains is the question every reader arrives at eventually: *so what do I build?*

The answer this study supports is specific enough to be uncomfortable: content approach appeared to explain outcomes more consistently than volume, topic selection, or execution quality in almost every case examined in the deep-dive set. The companies that grew through content compounding did so because of *what kind of content* they built, not because they built more of it, targeted better keywords, or executed well on the wrong things.

This chapter formalizes that philosophy into a single framework: five steps, applied in sequence, that describe how the best B2B brands built durable demand when the majority of their peers were losing traffic. It is not a checklist or a set of best practices. It is a decision framework derived from observing 118 companies over 18 months.

---

## The Durable Demand Framework

*[Figure 11.1 — The Durable Demand Framework. See visual notes at end of chapter.]*

**1. Diagnose the mechanism.**
Before any recovery or growth investment, identify which of the five mechanisms is operating. Traffic data tells you what happened; position data, timing, and volume data tell you why. The wrong treatment applied confidently is the most expensive outcome.

**2. Build engagement-requiring assets.**
Invest in content whose value requires the reader to engage (to read, think, and apply) rather than content that can be extracted and satisfied in a single SERP response. These are the two structural content categories this framework uses throughout: *engagement-requiring content* (content whose value is only accessible through continued engagement) and *satisfaction-enabling content* (content whose value can be fully extracted in a single interaction). Engagement-requiring content is structurally protected from AI interception. Satisfaction-enabling content is not.

**3. Diversify across durable content types.**
No single page above roughly 20% of gross gains. No single content type accounting for the majority of traffic. Distributed breadth across durable content types is what compounding looks like in organic programs that actually compound.

**4. Measure portfolio health.**
Cannibalization rate, position consolidation, branded/non-branded split, single-page concentration. These are measurable, observable signals that distinguish programs building compounding authority from programs running a content treadmill.

**5. Evaluate every investment with two questions.**
Before producing or maintaining any piece of content: does this create ongoing engagement? Can an AI system fully satisfy the query that would bring someone here without a click? If the answer to the second question is yes, the investment is structurally fragile, regardless of execution quality.

## The four durability tiers

The most consistent finding across the cohort is that content falls into four structural tiers based on how it holds up in the current environment. These tiers are observed from the data (confirmed across the growing companies in the deep-dive set, the declining companies, and the recovered companies), not derived from first principles.

### Tier 1 — Durable: Definitional and conceptual content

"What is X" pages covering foundational concepts in a domain. Confirmed durable across HR (Rippling), Finance (Navan, Ramp), DevOps (Harness), Security (Vanta), and multiple other categories. Definitional GRC content also anchored Hyperproof's recovery during the study window, confirming the pattern from the recovery side of the cohort, though that finding comes from a different evidence set than the five-company AIO-resistance data. This content type proved resistant to both AI Overview interception and algorithm volatility throughout the study window.

The explanation is enduring learning demand: the continuous arrival of new practitioners who need to understand foundational concepts for the first time. Every year, new engineers need to understand what continuous integration means in practice. Every year, companies begin the SOC 2 process for the first time. The concept doesn't change; the cohort of people who need to learn it replenishes.

### Tier 2 — Durable with conditions: Broad educational and strategic content

Methodology guides, comparison content, strategic frameworks. Durable when the content requires continued engagement to be useful: when the reader must read, think, and apply rather than skim and extract the useful part. Becomes fragile when it can be fully summarized by an AI Overview without the user needing to engage with the full piece.

The condition is everything. The same educational content can sit on either side of the line depending on depth, proprietary insight, and whether a surface-level summary accomplishes the reader's goal. This is the tier where the two-question test matters most.

### Tier 3 — Durable with structural defense: Proprietary methodology and original research

Content that doesn't exist anywhere else. Data, frameworks, and conclusions an AI cannot synthesize because no aggregated source contains them. This appears to be the most structurally defensible content type observed in this study: citation likelihood is high, direct replication is difficult (competitors can commission rival research, but cannot reproduce the same data), and the content compounds in authority the longer it exists.

The investment required is higher. The protection is commensurately stronger.

### Tier 4 — Fragile: Content whose value can be satisfied without engagement

Template and actionable-output content. Role, career, and title list content. Basic how-to content for well-documented, mature processes. Developer tutorials for established libraries.

What these content types share is a property: their primary value is something the user can use directly, without engaging deeply: an artifact, a list, a process step. AI tools are increasingly capable of generating that output directly. Aha!'s template content declined roughly 60% across 154 pages. LogRocket's developer tutorial traffic declined alongside the maturation of AI coding assistants. These are not isolated cases.

This category also includes any content whose primary value is satisfying a one-time SERP query entirely, where the user's goal is fully accomplished from the search results page, without a click, and without the content providing anything that requires further engagement.

## The two-question test

For every piece of content you're considering producing, maintaining, or investing in, ask two questions in sequence.

**Question 1: Does this create ongoing engagement?**

Will the user return to this content when their situation changes? Will they share it with colleagues? Does understanding it require reading, thinking, and applying, or can they extract the useful part and move on?

Ongoing engagement is the property that makes content compound. A practitioner who builds their methodology around a framework they discovered in your content, who shares it with a new hire, who returns to it as their team evolves: that user is developing a relationship with the content that extends beyond a single visit. That relationship is where authority accumulates.

**Question 2: Can an AI system fully satisfy the query that would bring them here without a click?**

Search the primary keyword in an incognito window. If an AI Overview appears, ask: can the user accomplish their goal from the Overview alone, not approximately, but fully? If they need to click through, the content has structural protection. If they don't, it doesn't, regardless of ranking position, length, or execution quality.

> **If the answer to Question 1 is yes and the answer to Question 2 is no, you have a durable content asset. That's the entire framework in two questions. Every other recommendation in this chapter is a way of applying that test at scale: to individual pages, to portfolios, and to future investment decisions.**

These two questions are the operational heart of this report. Everything that came before them (the mechanism diagnosis, the trajectory analysis, the AIO interception evidence) was preparation for being able to answer them accurately.

## What the winning programs had in common

Across the growing companies that built durable organic demand through content during the study window, five measurable portfolio signals appeared consistently. These are observations from programs that were already working, not strategic principles imposed from outside.

**Distributed gains, no dominant page.** No single page exceeded roughly 20% of gross gains. Braze, Harness, Navan, Vanta, and Ramp all showed this. The distribution was broad: dozens of pages each contributing modest individual amounts. This is what compounding distributed content looks like in the data: not one winner, but many contributors.

**Non-branded traffic as the primary growth driver.** Brand traffic grows as products grow. Content traffic grows as programs compound. The companies showing durable organic growth were generating it primarily from non-branded queries, attracting people who had never heard of the brand but had a question the content answered. This is the measure of organic program independence from brand momentum.

**Low cannibalization rate.** In the growing companies with clean signals, gross loss as a percentage of gross gain was consistently below 30%. In the declining programs, it was frequently above 60%, and in some cases above 100%, meaning the program was destroying more traffic than it created on a net basis. LogRocket showed 75.6% cannibalization; Aha! showed 286.6%. At those rates, publishing more content accelerates the problem.

**Position consolidation.** Positions 1-10 growing as a share of total rankings while positions 21-50 and 51+ contract. In the majority of the growing companies examined, this appeared alongside overall growth. The instinctive interpretation ("we're losing keywords") is wrong. The correct interpretation is that the program's strongest pages are moving into positions where traffic flows, while peripheral rankings that never converted are disappearing.

**Content type mix weighted toward Tiers 1-3.** The growing programs weren't exclusively definitional. But their mix was weighted toward content types that proved durable. Ramp combined a glossary (Tier 1) with free tools requiring ongoing engagement (Tier 2-3) and financial education content. Harness built primarily around DevOps concept pages. Vanta combined product-adjacent and compliance educational content. The through-line was a portfolio that didn't depend on Tier 4 content for its primary traffic.

Across all 38 full-weight classifiable growing companies, DE was the most common primary growth pattern (23 of 38 full-weight classifiable growers, 61%), ahead of brand-led growth (7), mixed growth patterns (7), and programmatic content (1). The stronger finding is the trajectory distribution: DE was the only bucket that appeared consistently across both favorable and adverse trajectories, including Growth then Pullback and Decline then Recovery, where brand-led growth was largely absent. The programs in this section sat across multiple buckets; what they shared was a content mix weighted toward types that held up when conditions turned adverse.

> **Research synthesis**
>
> *The five portfolio signals (distributed gains, non-branded growth driver, low cannibalization rate, position consolidation, durable content mix) are each individually measurable from a standard Ahrefs or Semrush export. Together, they distinguish programs building compounding authority from programs running a content treadmill that accelerates as the portfolio grows.*

## Investment decisions

This section describes three categories of content investment decision derived from the cohort evidence. They are designed to be printed, shared, and used as a filter against a content roadmap.

---

### STOP

*Investing in these content types for SEO purposes.*

**Template libraries and downloadable assets** whose primary value is the artifact, not the surrounding knowledge. AI tools generate these on demand. The traffic that used to flow to template pages was transactional: the transaction can now happen without a visit.

**Role, career, and title list content.** These queries are satisfiable in a single Overview paragraph. The click was never durable; it was a byproduct of the SERP's previous inability to answer the question directly.

**Basic how-to content for mature, well-documented processes.** If the process has been documented in existing guides, YouTube tutorials, and official documentation (and AI tools have been trained on all of it), you are producing supply into a market with collapsing demand. Refreshing this content does not restore volume that has migrated upstream.

---

### DE-RISK

*Continue, but restructure toward engagement-requiring depth.*

**Broad educational and strategic content** that currently sits in the middle of the satisfiability spectrum, valuable but potentially extractable. The response is not abandonment; it is depth. Add proprietary data. Add methodology layers an AI cannot synthesize from existing sources. Build the version that requires the reader to engage rather than skim.

**Content clusters where a significant share of traffic comes from satisfiable queries.** Identify the specific pages at risk using the SERP inspection protocol from Chapter 9. Make investment decisions per-page rather than per-cluster: the cluster may have durable assets mixed with fragile ones.

**Any content type adjacent to Tier 4 that currently performs.** Understand why it's still working, then decide whether the reason is durable or coincidental. Act before the signal changes, not after.

---

### DOUBLE DOWN

*These content types showed the strongest durability signals in the study.*

**Definitional and conceptual content** covering foundational knowledge at genuine depth. The evidence across five independent categories is unusually consistent. Build the version of these pages that answers the question a new professional in this domain actually has, not a search-engine-optimized summary, but the real answer.

**Proprietary methodology and original research.** Content that does not exist anywhere else. The investment required is higher; the structural protection is commensurately stronger. Every piece of original data your organization produces is a content asset that cannot be replicated or synthesized away.

**Free tools and interactive calculators** that require engagement to use. An AI can describe a budget framework; it cannot substitute for an interactive calculator processing the user's specific numbers. The engagement is the value.

**Entity-rich conceptual frameworks.** "What is X, how does X work, what are the components of X, how does X relate to Y", covered at depth, with proprietary explanation rather than topic overview. These are the pages AI systems retrieve and cite. These are the pages that rank and hold across algorithm updates and AIO expansion.

> **Research synthesis**
>
> *Aha!'s experience is the clearest evidence that execution quality does not substitute for structural durability. A mature, well-resourced content program with genuine category authority declined severely, because the investment was concentrated in Tier 4 content types. The most important implication: high effort and high quality cannot rescue a program built on content whose primary value is satisfiable without engagement.*

The companies that built durable demand built content that required engagement to be useful, and many of the companies that lost traffic depended on content that could be satisfied without a click.

## Before you move on

Set aside a half-day (or split across two working sessions) and run the audit. It produces four outputs you can act on immediately.

**Step 1:** Pull your Top Pages report and classify your top 50 pages against the four durability tiers: use **Figure 11.2** as your classification reference. This reveals the structural composition of your current program: how much of your traffic depends on content types that proved fragile in this study.

**Step 2:** From a 12-month Top Pages export, calculate your cannibalization rate: sum traffic increases across all pages (gross gain), sum traffic decreases (gross loss), divide gross loss by gross gain. Under 30%: healthy. 30–60%: moderate. Over 60%: treadmill. Over 100%: publishing more is accelerating the problem.

**Step 3:** For your top 10 pages by traffic (a narrower pass than the Chapter 9 diagnostic scan, focused here on the pages where satisfiability exposure most directly affects investment decisions), search the primary keyword in an incognito window and check for an AI Overview. If you ran the Chapter 9 inspection, pull those results and focus this step on your 10 highest-traffic pages with confirmed exposure. Apply Question 2 to each. Any page with confirmed satisfiability exposure representing more than 5% of total traffic is worth addressing before the loss becomes visible in your data.

**Step 4:** Apply the two-question test to every item on your current content roadmap. For each piece that fails Question 2, decide: can the content be restructured to require engagement? If yes, restructure before investing. If no, remove it from the roadmap.

**Figure 11.3** consolidates the five portfolio health metrics introduced across the report: cannibalization rate (Chapter 6), single-page concentration (Chapter 5), branded/non-branded split (Chapter 3), position distribution (Chapter 6), and content type mix (this chapter). If you have been running the diagnostics as you read, you already have most of these numbers. Figure 11.3 is where they come together.

At the end of those four steps, you have a stop list, a de-risk list, a double-down list, and a decision framework for every content investment going forward.

That's the entire report, applied.

---

Search didn't die. Fragile demand did.

The companies that continued growing in 2025 and 2026 weren't lucky, and they weren't early. They built content that required something from the reader (engagement, attention, application) rather than content that provided a quick answer and lost the user the moment the answer was given.

That's the difference between chasing traffic and building demand. Traffic can be intercepted. Demand (the kind that renews because the problem keeps recurring and the audience keeps arriving) cannot.

The organic landscape will keep shifting. AI capabilities will advance. Search behavior will continue to evolve. The programs that survive these shifts will be the ones that answered the two questions correctly: Does this require engagement? Can an AI satisfy it without a click?

Build the things that require engagement. That's what this report has been about from the first page.
