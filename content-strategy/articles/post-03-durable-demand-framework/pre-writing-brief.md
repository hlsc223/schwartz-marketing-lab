## Why this article exists

Evergreen content is the most widely recommended category of content strategy. But our research across 118 B2B SaaS companies shows that topic relevance alone doesn't determine whether content holds its traffic — the structure of demand underneath the content does. Most existing guides teach how to create evergreen content. None explain why some evergreen content still loses traffic. This article introduces the Durable Demand Framework as the model that explains what the current SERP doesn't.

## Central insight

*The one assumption-challenging sentence. The reader should be able to summarize the article from this sentence alone.*

> Plenty of evergreen content still loses traffic. The differentiator isn't topic relevance — it's whether the content creates demand that renews itself.

## Key stats and evidence to use

Pull precisely from `content-strategy/brand/stat-bank.md`. Use the exact figures — don't paraphrase loosely.

**The gap evidence (why evergreen ≠ durable):**
- Aha! template content: 154 template pages, net gross loss of −60.3%. Competitor analysis templates: −88.1%. Strategy templates: −89.8%. Creative brief templates: −96.4%. These are permanently relevant topics — the demand wasn't durable because AI systems deliver the output within the SERP.
- 63% of companies in the cohort failed to grow organic traffic (flat + declined).
- 30% of all companies tracked are currently more than 50% below their all-time organic traffic peak.

**The durable demand evidence (what actually worked):**
- 61% of the 38 full-weight classifiable growers grew primarily through definitional and educational content — "content covering foundational professional concepts professionals return to throughout their careers."
- This pattern appeared independently across five verticals: HR, DevOps, finance, security, and marketing automation.
- The distinguishing characteristic: "the content required sustained engagement to deliver its value — AI systems could not fully satisfy the query without the user reading the page."
- In no growing program did a single page account for more than roughly 20% of total gains — growth was distributed.
- Validated across 5 cases (Rippling, Navan, Ramp, Harness, Vanta): definitional content resisting AI Overview suppression while template/tool content collapsed.

**The citation confirmation:**
- Blog/educational articles: 5 of 9 confirmed AI citations in the cohort. Product pages and marketing content: fewest.
- The same content types that held in search also received the most AI citations.

**The impressions/clicks divergence (Gladly):**
- Over the study window, Gladly's organic impressions grew 47.7% while organic clicks fell 41.4% — with average positions steady or slightly improving. The top impression-generating pages: FAQ templates (896,401 impressions, 0.05% CTR), customer service scripts, job descriptions. This is the aggregate-account-level version of what the Aha! keyword table shows at the query level. Content was indexed, ranking, and visible — but AI systems were resolving the query before the click. Use as a paired example with Aha! to show what non-durable demand looks like in the data: growing visibility, shrinking clicks.

**The Harness AIO-resistance case:**
- Harness holds position #1 for "what is a devops pipeline" even when Google displays an AI Overview on the SERP. Described in the research as "the strongest keyword-level AI-resistance evidence in the cohort." The query is definitional — exactly the type that triggers AI Overviews — but the page generates substantial click traffic despite the AI summary being present. Use as the clearest evidence for the "requires engagement" characteristic: Google tried to answer the question in the SERP, and users still clicked through, because understanding a DevOps pipeline requires reading an actual explainer.

## Argument structure

The article's logical progression — what the reader must believe at each stage for the final belief shift to land.

**1. Establish the common belief**
Evergreen content is a widely understood and genuinely useful concept. Topic relevance matters. There's nothing wrong with this thinking — it's just incomplete. (Don't spend 800 words here. One tight paragraph establishes the premise; a brief pivot ends it.)

**2. Introduce the problem with the evidence**
Our research across 118 B2B SaaS companies found that some of the most "evergreen" content still lost significant traffic. The issue wasn't topic expiration — competitor analysis templates, strategy planning guides, and product roadmap tools are permanently relevant topics. They lost 88-96% of their traffic because AI systems could satisfy the query within the SERP. Topic relevance is necessary. It isn't sufficient.

**3. Introduce the Durable Demand Framework — the central distinction**
The Durable Demand Framework centers on one distinction: **engagement-requiring content** vs. **satisfaction-enabling content**.

- **Engagement-requiring content** — content whose value is only accessible through continued engagement. The reader must read, think, and apply. A "what is a DevOps pipeline" explainer doesn't deliver its value in a paragraph — it requires the reader to work through it. This content is structurally protected from AI interception because the value can't be extracted without the click.
- **Satisfaction-enabling content** — content whose value can be fully extracted in a single SERP interaction. A template. A job description list. A basic how-to for a mature process. The user's goal is accomplished without engaging with the content itself. This content is structurally fragile regardless of execution quality or topic relevance.

The practical test is the **two-question test** from the research:
1. Does this create ongoing engagement — does the user need to read, think, and apply?
2. Can an AI system fully satisfy the query that would bring someone here without a click?

> If the answer to question 1 is yes and the answer to question 2 is no, you have a durable content asset.

**4. Explain why definitional content is the clearest case of engagement-requiring demand**
The reason "what is X" content held up across five independent verticals isn't just topic relevance. It's structural: every year, a new cohort of practitioners needs to understand foundational concepts for the first time. The audience replenishes continuously. The concept doesn't change. This is what durable demand looks like — not a one-time query, but a query that recurs because the people asking it keep arriving. Definitional content captures that recurring demand. Template content captures a one-time transaction.

**5. Show what this looks like in practice**
Contrast the Aha! template library vs. the Harness DevOps content cluster. Aha!'s template pages lost 88-96% of their traffic — the query was satisfiable in the SERP. Harness holds #1 for "what is a devops pipeline" even when Google displays an AI Overview — the answer requires engagement, so users still click through. Same "evergreen" label by conventional definition; opposite structural properties.

**6. The portfolio implication**
Programs built on durable demand look different in the data: gains distributed across dozens of pages, no single page above ~20%, non-branded traffic leading growth. This isn't a coincidence — it's what a program built on renewing audience demand looks like. Many queries, each modest, each refreshed by the continuous arrival of people asking them for the first time.

**7. Reframe the evaluation question**
The existing question: "Is this topic evergreen?" (Will it stay relevant over time?)
The better question: "Does satisfying this query require the reader to engage with the content? Or can they accomplish their goal from the SERP alone?" These questions look similar but produce very different content portfolios.
