# Evidence

- [x] Original research — 118-company cohort, 18-month window (the five-mechanism taxonomy was developed from patterns observed across the cohort and subsequent company-level analysis; do not claim the five mechanisms "account for the majority of declines" without a mechanism-count calculation from the repository)
- [x] Research repository — named case companies per mechanism:
  - Algorithmic Reassessment: CloudBees and Ramp (August 2025 spam update — timing aligns with a named update, the more specific subtype; the article should note that in some cases timing maps to a named update, in others the affected pattern is clear but the responsible system cannot be isolated)
  - AI Overview Interception: Aha! (20+ queries, rankings held or improved, traffic −80–99%)
  - Architectural Damage: Freshworks (December 2025 "Dew" design system launch)
  - AI Tool Demand Substitution: Aha! template content + LogRocket developer tutorials
  - Competitive Displacement: requires positive evidence that a competitor, first-party source, directory, or replacement result type captured visibility the subject lost. It may appear near the end of the decision tree, but it should not be assigned solely because the other mechanisms were eliminated.

**Note on Aha! dual-mechanism use:** Aha! appears under both AIO Interception and AI Tool Demand Substitution. These were different page cohorts within the same domain experiencing different mechanisms. The article must make this explicit: a single domain can experience more than one mechanism simultaneously; the framework classifies loss patterns, not companies as indivisible units. This is also the place to introduce the primary mechanism discipline — identify the mechanism explaining the largest share of decline, note contributing mechanisms separately.
- [x] Decision tree (Figure 4.2) — the article's primary shareable asset; renders the diagnostic process in a single scannable visual
- [ ] Client observations — not in this article
- [ ] GSC/GA4 data — not available; Ahrefs-based evidence throughout

**Key evidence to lead with:** The Aha! same-position CTR collapse (20+ queries, ranked #1 or improved, traffic fell 80–99%) is the sharpest proof that mechanism identification matters. If you don't know what you're looking for, you'd see ranking stability and think there's no problem. There is. It's the wrong mechanism.

# Risks

- **Summarizing the chapter, not writing the article.** Chapter 4 is a report chapter. The blog post needs to be written for someone who hasn't read the report — no assumed context, no "as we saw in Chapter 1" references.
- **Burying the decision tree.** The Figure 4.2 decision tree is the post's most shareable and citable asset. It should appear early enough that a reader who stops halfway through still encountered it. Don't save it for the end.
- **Five equal sections without a diagnostic hierarchy.** The mechanisms are not equally easy to distinguish. AIO Interception and Algorithmic Reassessment are especially easy to confuse because both can coincide with declining traffic, while Architectural Damage usually has a clearer implementation signal. Competitive Displacement requires affirmative replacement evidence. The article should reflect these diagnostic differences rather than presenting five interchangeable causes.
- **Over-relying on chapter language.** Chapter 4 uses the emoji icons and structured card format appropriate for a PDF. The blog post needs to be prose-first, with the framework embedded in the argument — not five identical structured blocks.
- **Losing the "Mechanism > Symptom" thesis.** The post's entire argument rests on the claim that most companies skip diagnosis and apply the wrong treatment. That claim needs to be made explicitly and backed with evidence (the Aha! example is the proof case) — not implied.
