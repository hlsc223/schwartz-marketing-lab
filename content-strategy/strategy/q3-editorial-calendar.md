## Organizing principle

Posts are sequenced to build a mental model in the reader, not to publish the report in chapter order. Proprietary models (Five Mechanisms, Durable Demand Framework, Rankings ≠ Traffic) come first — they become the citation anchors everything else links to. Definitional/comparison posts come after the worldview is established, so the distinctions land with context. Tactical and downstream posts come last.

Every post should use S.LAB's proprietary vocabulary consistently. See `content-strategy/brand/brand-point-of-view.md` → Proprietary Vocabulary.

## Notes on evaluation

**Don't evaluate all posts by the same metric.** Each post has a primary job:

- **Press / links / awareness** (Weeks 1, 7): Measure by pickups, backlinks, shares.
- **Establish methodology / sales enablement** (Weeks 2, 3, 13, 14): Measure by internal link usage, sales team referrals, citation by prospects.
- **Own proprietary framework / earn citations** (Weeks 3, 4): Measure by AI citation frequency, backlinks, how often other content links to them.
- **Rank / start the clock** (Weeks 5, 6, 10, 12): Measure by keyword rankings and organic traffic 90–180 days out.
- **Capture demand / convert** (Weeks 10, 12, 13): Measure by qualified leads, CTA clicks, playbook downloads.

## Repository-driven content (ongoing)

After Wave 1, the research repository should actively generate editorial topics. When new company migrations produce surprising findings — an unexpected mechanism, a sixth pattern, a recovery archetype not yet documented — those become posts. This is the long-term content engine: competitors brainstorm topics; S.LAB discovers them from data.

Questions to ask after every 10–20 company migrations:
- What surprised us?
- Did any company break the existing model?
- Did we observe a pattern that isn't yet in the Durable Demand Framework?
- Is there a mechanism interaction we haven't written about?

If yes to any: that's the next blog.
