Evergreen content is a genuinely useful concept. Content on topics that stay relevant should keep generating traffic. The logic is straightforward, and for most of content marketing's history, it worked.

It still works — for some content. For other content that follows the same logic, traffic has collapsed. Same "evergreen" label. Opposite results.

Evergreen content is supposed to keep producing traffic because the topic stays relevant. Our [research across 118 B2B SaaS companies](/blog/b2b-organic-traffic-split-2025) suggests that's only part of the story.

## The topic didn't expire. The demand structure did.

Aha! is a product management software company with a content program that was, by conventional metrics, built correctly. The company's blog covered product management topics at scale. The template library offered competitor analysis templates, product roadmap templates, creative brief templates — permanently relevant topics, carefully produced.

Between January 2025 and June 2026, 154 of those template pages lost an average of 60.3% of their traffic. Competitor analysis templates: −88.1%. Strategy templates: −89.8%. Creative brief templates: −96.4%.

The topics didn't expire. Companies still need competitor analysis frameworks. Product teams still use creative briefs. The decline happened because Google's AI systems could now deliver a template — or the framework behind one — directly in the search results. The query was being satisfied without a click.

The same pattern appeared differently at Gladly, a customer service platform. Over the same window, Gladly's organic impressions grew 47.7% while organic clicks fell 41.4%, with no meaningful change in ranking positions. The site became more visible in search while receiving fewer visits. The content generating the most impressions: FAQ templates, customer service scripts, job descriptions.

Topic relevance is necessary to appear in search. It isn't what determines whether users click through.

## The one property that separates durable content from fragile content

The companies that maintained and grew organic traffic through the same window had something in common that had nothing to do with the topics they covered. Their content required engagement to be useful.

That's the distinction the Durable Demand Framework is built on: **engagement-requiring content** versus **satisfaction-enabling content**.

Engagement-requiring content is content whose value is only accessible through continued engagement. The reader must read, think, and apply — not extract and move on. A guide that explains how a DevOps pipeline actually works, how to build a GRC compliance program, what makes a risk management framework appropriate for a specific company context: these deliver their value through the reading, not from a paragraph summary.

Satisfaction-enabling content is content whose value can be fully extracted in a single interaction. A template. A list of job description examples. A step-by-step process for a well-documented task. The user's goal is accomplished without engaging with the content at depth. That's exactly what AI systems can now handle inside the SERP.

The practical test is two questions, applied in sequence:

1. **Does this create ongoing engagement?** Does the reader need to read, think, and apply — or can they extract what they need and move on?
2. **Can an AI system fully satisfy the query that would bring someone here without a click?** Search the primary keyword in an incognito window. If an AI Overview appears, ask honestly: can the user accomplish their goal from that Overview alone — not approximately, but fully?

If the answer to question 1 is yes and the answer to question 2 is no, you have a durable content asset.

Aha!'s template content fails both tests. A competitor analysis template delivers its value as an artifact — the user extracts it and moves on, and AI can now generate that artifact on demand. The content was never engagement-requiring in the first place. The Durable Demand Framework would have flagged it as structurally fragile before any algorithm changed.

## Why definitional content keeps producing traffic

Harness, a DevOps platform, holds position #1 for "what is a devops pipeline" even when Google displays an AI Overview on the SERP for that query. That's not a ranking anomaly. It's a signal about the content's structural properties: understanding a DevOps pipeline requires reading an explainer, not extracting a definition. Google tried to answer the question in the SERP. Users still clicked through.

That pattern held across five independent verticals in our research: HR (Rippling), finance (Navan, Ramp), DevOps (Harness), security (Vanta), and marketing automation (Braze). In each case, definitional and educational content grew through the same window that collapsed Aha!'s template library and Gladly's script pages.

The reason is structural. Every year, a new cohort of practitioners needs to understand foundational concepts for the first time. New engineers need to understand what continuous integration means in practice. Companies begin SOC 2 compliance programs they've never run before. The concept doesn't change — but the people who need to understand it keep arriving.

That continuous arrival is what makes this category of demand durable. Template content captures a one-time transaction. Foundational educational content captures a query that keeps renewing as the audience replenishes.

This is what the framework means by durable demand. Not content on topics that stay relevant. Content on topics where the demand keeps arriving because the people asking the question keep arriving.

## What this looks like across 118 companies

Across the [38 companies in our cohort](/blog/b2b-organic-traffic-split-2025) that we could fully classify by primary growth driver, 61% grew primarily through definitional and educational content. That pattern appeared independently across five verticals — which matters: it wasn't a category-wide tailwind. Growing and declining companies coexisted in the same categories over the same window.

The programs that grew also shared a consistent portfolio shape: no single page accounted for more than roughly 20% of gross traffic gains. Growth was distributed across dozens of pages, each contributing a modest individual amount. That's what a program built on renewing audience demand looks like in the data — not one winning page, but many queries, each refreshed by the continuous arrival of people asking them for the first time.

63% of the 118 companies in our study failed to grow organic traffic over the 18-month window. For most of them, the problem wasn't execution quality or topic selection. They had content on relevant topics. They published consistently. But the underlying content was built to capture one-time queries — and one-time queries are exactly what AI systems are now absorbing.

## Two questions that replace the evergreen test

The evergreen framework asks: "Will this topic stay relevant?" That's a useful filter, but it screens for the wrong property. Relevance is a necessary condition for appearing in search. It isn't what determines whether users stay, click, and return.

| | Evergreen thinking | Durable Demand thinking |
|---|---|---|
| **Core question** | Will this topic be searched in 3 years? | Will the people asking this question keep arriving? |
| **What holds traffic** | Topic permanence | Engagement requirement |
| **Primary risk** | Topic expires | AI satisfies the query without a click |
| **How to evaluate a page** | Is the keyword still searched? | Can an AI answer it without a click? |
| **Portfolio goal** | Coverage of lasting topics | Content mix weighted toward engagement-requiring assets |

The shift isn't from evergreen content to some different category of content. It's from a question about topic longevity to a question about demand structure. Some evergreen topics have durable demand underneath them. Others don't. The topic tells you very little about which category you're in. The two-question test does.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is all definitional content automatically durable?

No. Definitional content is the clearest example of engagement-requiring demand, but the property that makes it durable is the engagement requirement, not the format. A surface-level "what is X" page that can be fully answered in two sentences of an AI Overview isn't structurally durable regardless of the topic. The test is always question 2: can an AI fully satisfy the query without a click? Depth, proprietary framing, and genuine conceptual explanation determine the answer.

### What does satisfaction-enabling content look like in practice?

Templates and downloadable artifacts whose primary value is the output, not the thinking behind it. Role, career, and title list content. Basic how-to content for well-documented processes. Any content where the user's goal is fully accomplished from the search result without needing to read the page. The format isn't the signal — the structural property is. A long-form guide can be satisfaction-enabling if its primary value is a list the user copies and moves on.

### How do I apply the two-question test to my existing content?

Search your primary keyword in an incognito window. If an AI Overview appears, ask honestly: can someone accomplish their goal from that Overview alone — not approximately, but fully? If yes, the content is structurally vulnerable regardless of ranking position or execution quality. If the Overview prompts a follow-up click to understand the concept better, that's a signal the content is engagement-requiring. Run this test on your top 10 pages by traffic first, then your roadmap.

### Can content that passes the test today fail it later?

Yes. As AI systems improve, the threshold for "fully satisfying a query" shifts. Content that required engagement to understand in 2022 may be satisfiable by a well-trained model today. This isn't a reason to avoid building engagement-requiring content — it's a reason to weight the portfolio toward the kinds of questions that require genuine expertise to answer well, where the engagement requirement is driven by complexity and depth rather than the current limits of AI capability.

### Does this mean I should stop publishing how-to content?

Not categorically. The question is whether the how-to requires engagement to be useful. A guide to building a risk management framework for a company encountering compliance requirements for the first time — one that requires reading, thinking, and applying to a specific context — can be deeply engagement-requiring. A step-by-step process for a mature task that AI can walk someone through in a chat window is not. The format doesn't determine the answer. The depth and specificity of the engagement required does.
