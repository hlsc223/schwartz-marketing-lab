Evergreen content is a genuinely useful concept. Content on topics that stay relevant should keep generating traffic. The logic is straightforward, and for most of content marketing's history, it worked.

It still works — for some content. For other content that follows the same logic, traffic has collapsed. Same "evergreen" label. Opposite results.

Evergreen content is supposed to keep producing traffic because the topic stays relevant. Our [research across 118 B2B SaaS companies](/blog/b2b-organic-traffic-split-2025) suggests that's only part of the story.

## The topic didn't expire. The demand structure did.

Research across 118 B2B SaaS companies over an 18-month window found that 47% of companies experienced significant traffic decline. Over 30% of declining companies lost over half of their organic traffic. 

For example, Aha! is a product management software company with a content program that was, by conventional metrics, built correctly. Its blog covered product management topics at scale. The template library offered competitor analysis templates, product roadmap templates, creative brief templates — tons of carefully produced evergreen content.

But during the study window, 154 of those template pages lost an average of 60% of their traffic. Competitor analysis templates lost 88%. Strategy templates lost 90%. Creative brief templates lost 96%.

The topics didn't expire. Companies still need competitor analysis frameworks and product teams still use creative briefs. So, what happened?

**The decline happened because Google's AI systems could now deliver the content directly in the search results.** The query was being satisfied without a click.

The same pattern appeared differently at Gladly, a customer service platform. Over the same window, Gladly's organic impressions grew 48% while organic clicks fell 41%, with no meaningful change in ranking positions. The site became more visible in search while receiving fewer visits. The content generating the most impressions: FAQ templates, customer service scripts, job descriptions.

While many companies have experienced severe traffic loss since 2025, **roughly 37% of companies studied grew consistently.** 

By closely examing what the biggest traffic winners and losers had in common, we established the Durable Demand framework for how to develop an evergreen content strategy that holds up in 2026 and beyond. 

The Durable Demand framework at a glance

The Durable Demand Framework is a five-step decision methodology for building and evaluating a content protfolio that holds traffc as algorithms change, AI systems intercept traffic, and new competirors enter the fold. 

The five steps run in sequence: 



### Step 1: Diagnose before you prescribe

There are five main reasons your organic traffic might decline: 

1. Google Algorithm updates: Google penalizes your content
2. AI Overview interception: Goolge's AI overviews answer the users' question before they even visit a website at all
3. AI tool substitution: The user handled everything within an answer engine like ChatGPT, Claude, or Perplexity. 
4. Architectural damage: An internal change to your website broke something and hurt your standing with Google or AI search crawlers.
5. Competitive displacement: New or existing competitors started outranking your on your target keywords. 

It may not be one mechanism behind your traffic decline. In fact, there are likely multiple culprits for brands experiencing decline. The first step is understanding the problem, so you know what you have to fight against. 

Don't skip this step if your traffic isn't declining. You may be operating on borrowed time. It's still worthwhile to do a full content audit to make sure your content isn't built on a creaky foundation. 

### Step 2: Build content around continuous engagement

Content that satisfies a simple question is weak. AI and competitors can both do that job and the Google algorithm doesn't respect it. 

As a starting point, think about your content as engagement-requiring content versus satisfaction-enabling content. 

**Engagement-requiring content** is content whose value is only accessible through continued engagement. The reader must read, think, and apply — not extract and move on. A guide that explains how a DevOps pipeline actually works, how to build a GRC compliance program, what makes a risk management framework appropriate for a specific company context: these deliver their value through the reading, not from a paragraph summary.

**Satisfaction-enabling content** is content whose value can be fully extracted in a single interaction. A template. A list of job description examples. A step-by-step process for a well-documented task. The user's goal is accomplished without engaging with the content at depth. That's exactly what AI systems can now handle inside the SERP.

That requires a shift from evergreen content thinking to durable demand thinking.

### Step 3: Maintain a diverse web traffic portfolio

Having one page go gangbusters and driving a ton of traffic is great. But is it sustainable? Probably not. 

The companies that grew organic traffic through the study window didn't just build the right individual pieces. Their portfolios had a consistent shape.

**No single page exceeded roughly 20% of gross traffic gains.** Growth was distributed across dozens of pages, each contributing a modest individual amount — Braze, Harness, Navan, Vanta, and Ramp all showed this. That's what compounding distributed content looks like in the data: not one winning page, but many queries, each refreshed by the continuous arrival of people asking them for the first time.

### Step 4: Nurture portfolio health

**Cannibalization rate** — gross loss divided by gross gain. Below 30%: healthy. Above 60%: the program is running a treadmill. Above 100%: publishing more is accelerating the problem. LogRocket showed 75.6% cannibalization during the study window. Aha! showed 286.6%. At that rate, more content makes the situation worse.

**Single-page concentration** — if one page accounts for more than roughly 20% of total traffic gains, the program is fragile by construction. Durable programs distribute.

**Non-branded growth** — brand traffic grows as products grow. Content traffic grows as programs compound. The distinction is the measure of a program's independence from brand momentum.

**Position consolidation** — positions 1–10 growing as a share of total rankings while positions 21–50 contract is healthy, not alarming. Peripheral rankings that never converted are disappearing. Strong pages are moving into positions where traffic flows.

### Step 5: The two-question test

The two-question test is the entry point into the framework — the operational way the classification logic in Step 2 gets applied to real decisions. For every piece of content you're considering producing, maintaining, or investing in:

**Question 1: Does this create ongoing engagement?** Does the user need to read, think, and apply — or can they extract what they need and move on?

**Question 2: Can an AI system fully satisfy the query that would bring someone here without a click?** Search the primary keyword in an incognito window. If an AI Overview appears, ask honestly: can the user accomplish their goal from that Overview alone — not approximately, but fully?

> If the answer to Question 1 is yes and the answer to Question 2 is no, you have a durable content asset.

## What to do with what you find

Once you've classified your content against the four durability tiers, three investment decisions follow directly from the data.

**STOP** investing — for SEO purposes — in template libraries and downloadable assets whose primary value is the artifact itself; role, career, and title list content; basic how-to content for mature, well-documented processes. Refreshing Tier 4 content does not restore traffic that has moved into the SERP. It accelerates a treadmill.

**De-risk** by restructuring: broad educational content that currently sits in the satisfiability middle ground. Add proprietary data. Add methodology layers an AI cannot synthesize from existing sources. The question isn't whether to abandon it — it's whether the content can be restructured from Tier 2 toward Tier 3. Per-page inspection produces more accurate results than broad category decisions.

**Double down** on: definitional and conceptual content covering foundational knowledge at genuine depth; proprietary methodology and original research; free tools and interactive calculators that require engagement to use; entity-rich conceptual frameworks where the engagement requirement is driven by complexity and expertise, not by the current limits of AI capability. These are the content types that held up or grew across 18 months and five independent verticals. The evidence is unusually consistent.

63% of the 118 companies in our study failed to grow organic traffic over the 18-month window. For most of them, the problem wasn't execution quality or topic selection. They had content on relevant topics. They published consistently. The underlying content was built to capture one-time queries. One-time queries are exactly what AI systems are now absorbing.

**The topic didn't expire. The demand structure did.**

Evergreen content wasn't wrong. It was incomplete.

For years, topic relevance was a sufficient filter because search engines were retrieval systems. They matched queries to content. AI changed the economics of retrieval. A system that can generate a competitor analysis template on demand doesn't need to send users to a page that offers one. The demand is still there. The mechanism for satisfying it changed.

The competitive advantage is no longer publishing content on topics that stay relevant. It's publishing content whose value survives even after the answer exists somewhere else. That's what durable demand measures. Not topics that last. Demand that renews — because the problem keeps recurring, and the audience keeps arriving.

## Why definitional content keeps producing traffic

Harness, a DevOps platform, holds position #1 for "what is a devops pipeline" even when Google displays an AI Overview on the SERP for that query. That's not a ranking anomaly. It's a signal about the content's structural properties: understanding a DevOps pipeline requires reading an explainer, not extracting a definition. Google tried to answer the question in the SERP. Users still clicked through.

That pattern held across five independent verticals in our research: HR (Rippling), finance (Navan, Ramp), DevOps (Harness), security (Vanta), and marketing automation (Braze). In each case, definitional and educational content grew through the same window that collapsed Aha!'s template library and Gladly's script pages.

The reason is structural. Every year, a new cohort of practitioners needs to understand foundational concepts for the first time. New engineers need to understand what continuous integration means in practice. Companies begin SOC 2 compliance programs they've never run before. The concept doesn't change — but the people who need to understand it keep arriving.

That continuous arrival is what makes this category of demand durable. Template content captures a one-time transaction. Foundational educational content captures a query that keeps renewing as the audience replenishes.

This is what the framework means by durable demand. Not content on topics that stay relevant. Content on topics where the demand keeps arriving because the people asking the question keep arriving.

## Two questions that replace the evergreen test

The evergreen framework asks: "Will this topic stay relevant?" That's a useful filter, but it screens for the wrong property. Relevance is a necessary condition for appearing in search. It isn't what determines whether users stay, click, and return.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is all definitional content automatically durable?

No. Definitional content is the clearest example of engagement-requiring demand, but the property that makes it durable is the engagement requirement, not the format. A surface-level "what is X" page that can be fully answered in two sentences of an AI Overview isn't structurally durable regardless of the topic. The test is always question 2: can an AI fully satisfy the query without a click? Depth, proprietary framing, and genuine conceptual explanation determine the answer.

### What does satisfaction-enabling content look like in practice?

Templates and downloadable artifacts whose primary value is the output, not the thinking behind it. Role, career, and title list content. Basic how-to content for well-documented processes. Any content where the user's goal is fully accomplished from the search result without needing to read the page. The format isn't the signal — the structural property is. A long-form guide can be satisfaction-enabling if its primary value is a list the user copies and moves on.

### How do I apply the two-question test to my existing content?

Search your primary keyword in an incognito window. If an AI Overview appears, ask honestly: can someone accomplish their goal from that Overview alone — not approximately, but fully? If yes, the content is structurally vulnerable regardless of ranking position or execution quality. If the Overview prompts a follow-up click to understand the concept better, that's a signal the content is engagement-requiring. Run this test on your top 10 pages by traffic first, then your roadmap.

### Can content that passes the test today fail it later?

Yes. As AI systems improve, the threshold for "fully satisfying a query" shifts. Content that required engagement to understand in 2022 may be satisfiable by a well-trained model today. This isn't a reason to avoid building engagement-requiring content — it's a reason to weight the portfolio toward the kinds of questions that require genuine expertise to answer well, where the engagement requirement is driven by complexity and depth rather than the current limits of AI capability.

### Does this mean I should stop publishing how-to content?

Not categorically. The question is whether the how-to requires engagement to be useful. A guide to building a risk management framework for a company encountering compliance requirements for the first time — one that requires reading, thinking, and applying to a specific context — can be deeply engagement-requiring. A step-by-step process for a mature task that AI can walk someone through in a chat window is not. The format doesn't determine the answer. The depth and specificity of the engagement required does.
