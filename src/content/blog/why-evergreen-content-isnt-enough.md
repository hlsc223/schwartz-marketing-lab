Evergreen content is a genuinely useful concept. Content on topics that stay relevant should keep generating traffic. The logic is straightforward, and for most of content marketing's history, it worked.

It still works — for some content. For other content that follows the same logic, traffic has collapsed. Same "evergreen" label. Opposite results.

Evergreen content is supposed to keep producing traffic because the topic stays relevant. Our [research across 118 B2B SaaS companies](/blog/b2b-organic-traffic-split-2025) suggests that's only part of the story.

## The topic didn't expire. The demand structure did.

Aha! is a product management software company with a content program that was, by conventional metrics, built correctly. The company's blog covered product management topics at scale. The template library offered competitor analysis templates, product roadmap templates, creative brief templates — permanently relevant topics, carefully produced.

Between January 2025 and June 2026, 154 of those template pages lost an average of 60.3% of their traffic. Competitor analysis templates: −88.1%. Strategy templates: −89.8%. Creative brief templates: −96.4%.

The topics didn't expire. Companies still need competitor analysis frameworks. Product teams still use creative briefs. The demand was always there — and still is. What changed was how that demand gets satisfied. Google's AI systems can now deliver a template, or the framework behind one, directly in the search results. The query resolves without a click.

The same pattern appeared differently at Gladly, a customer service platform. Over the same window, Gladly's organic impressions grew 47.7% while organic clicks fell 41.4%, with no meaningful change in ranking positions. The site became more visible in search while receiving fewer visits. The content generating the most impressions: FAQ templates, customer service scripts, job descriptions.

Topic relevance is what gets content indexed. It is not what determines whether users click through. That distinction has always been true. AI changed the economics of it.

The topic didn't expire. The demand structure did.

## The Durable Demand Framework at a glance

The Durable Demand Framework is a five-step decision methodology for building and evaluating a content portfolio that holds traffic as AI systems take on more of what search engines used to do. The five steps run in sequence.

| Step | What it does | The decision it enables |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Diagnose the mechanism** | Identify which of the five mechanisms is operating before any investment | Which treatment to apply |
| **2. Build engagement-requiring assets** | Invest in content whose value requires reading, thinking, and applying | What kind of content to build |
| **3. Diversify across durable content types** | Weight the portfolio toward Tiers 1–3; no single page dominant | How to structure the program |
| **4. Measure portfolio health** | Track cannibalization rate, position consolidation, branded/non-branded split | Whether the program is compounding or running a treadmill |
| **5. Evaluate every investment** | Apply the two-question test before producing or maintaining any content | Whether to build, restructure, or stop |

### Step 1: Diagnose before you prescribe

Traffic can decline for five distinct reasons — algorithm update, AI Overview interception, link loss, competitor displacement, or content model mismatch — and each requires a different response. Applying the wrong treatment confidently is the most expensive mistake a content program can make.

The [five mechanisms behind organic traffic decline](/blog/organic-traffic-drop-mechanisms) are covered in detail separately. The point here is sequencing: the rest of this framework assumes you know which mechanism you're dealing with before you start rebuilding.

---

### Step 2: Build engagement-requiring assets

This is the framework's core distinction.

**Engagement-requiring content** is content whose value is only accessible through continued engagement. The reader must read, think, and apply — not extract and move on. A guide that explains how a DevOps pipeline actually works, how to build a GRC compliance program, what makes a risk management framework appropriate for a specific company context: these deliver their value through the reading, not from a paragraph summary.

**Satisfaction-enabling content** is content whose value can be fully extracted in a single interaction. A template. A list of job description examples. A step-by-step process for a well-documented task. The user's goal is accomplished without engaging with the content at depth. That's exactly what AI systems can now handle inside the SERP.

The framework classifies content against four durability tiers based on this distinction:

**Tier 1 — Durable: Definitional and conceptual content.** "What is X" pages covering foundational concepts in a domain. This content type proved resistant to both AI Overview interception and algorithm volatility throughout the study window — confirmed across HR (Rippling), finance (Navan, Ramp), DevOps (Harness), security (Vanta), and marketing automation (Braze). The explanation is enduring learning demand: every year, a new cohort of practitioners needs to understand foundational concepts for the first time. The concept doesn't change. The cohort that needs to learn it replenishes continuously.

**Tier 2 — Durable with conditions: Broad educational and strategic content.** Methodology guides, comparison content, strategic frameworks. Durable when the content requires continued engagement to be useful — when the reader must read, think, and apply rather than skim and extract. Becomes fragile when a surface-level AI Overview can fully accomplish the reader's goal. The condition is everything. This is the tier where the two-question test (Step 5) matters most.

**Tier 3 — Durable with structural defense: Proprietary methodology and original research.** Content that doesn't exist anywhere else. Data, frameworks, and conclusions an AI cannot synthesize because no aggregated source contains them. Citation likelihood is high. Direct replication is difficult. The investment required is higher. The structural protection is commensurately stronger.

**Tier 4 — Fragile: Content whose value can be satisfied without engagement.** Templates and downloadable artifacts. Role, career, and title list content. Basic how-to content for well-documented, mature processes. What these share is a structural property: their primary value is something the user can use directly, without engaging deeply. AI tools are increasingly capable of generating that output inside the SERP. Aha!'s template content declined roughly 60% across 154 pages. LogRocket's developer tutorial traffic declined alongside the maturation of AI coding assistants. These are not isolated cases.

Harness holds position #1 for "what is a devops pipeline" even when Google displays an AI Overview on the SERP. That's not a ranking anomaly. Google tried to answer the question in the SERP. Users still clicked through, because understanding a DevOps pipeline requires reading an actual explainer. That's Tier 1 working exactly as the framework predicts.

### Steps 3 and 4: Portfolio structure and health

The companies that grew organic traffic through the study window didn't just build the right individual pieces. Their portfolios had a consistent shape.

No single page exceeded roughly 20% of gross traffic gains. Growth was distributed across dozens of pages, each contributing a modest individual amount — Braze, Harness, Navan, Vanta, and Ramp all showed this. That's what compounding distributed content looks like in the data: not one winning page, but many queries, each refreshed by the continuous arrival of people asking them for the first time.

Portfolio health is measurable from a standard Ahrefs or Semrush export:

**Cannibalization rate** — gross loss divided by gross gain. Below 30%: healthy. Above 60%: the program is running a treadmill. Above 100%: publishing more is accelerating the problem. LogRocket showed 75.6% cannibalization during the study window. Aha! showed 286.6%. At that rate, more content makes the situation worse.

**Single-page concentration** — if one page accounts for more than roughly 20% of total traffic gains, the program is fragile by construction. Durable programs distribute.

**Non-branded growth** — brand traffic grows as products grow. Content traffic grows as programs compound. The distinction is the measure of a program's independence from brand momentum.

**Position consolidation** — positions 1–10 growing as a share of total rankings while positions 21–50 contract is healthy, not alarming. Peripheral rankings that never converted are disappearing. Strong pages are moving into positions where traffic flows.

---

### Step 5: The two-question test

The two-question test is the entry point into the framework — the operational way the classification logic in Step 2 gets applied to real decisions. For every piece of content you're considering producing, maintaining, or investing in:

**Question 1: Does this create ongoing engagement?** Does the user need to read, think, and apply — or can they extract what they need and move on?

**Question 2: Can an AI system fully satisfy the query that would bring someone here without a click?** Search the primary keyword in an incognito window. If an AI Overview appears, ask honestly: can the user accomplish their goal from that Overview alone — not approximately, but fully?

> If the answer to Question 1 is yes and the answer to Question 2 is no, you have a durable content asset.

## What to do with what you find

Once you've classified your content against the four durability tiers, three investment decisions follow directly from the data.

**STOP** investing — for SEO purposes — in template libraries and downloadable assets whose primary value is the artifact itself; role, career, and title list content; basic how-to content for mature, well-documented processes. Refreshing Tier 4 content does not restore traffic that has moved into the SERP. It accelerates a treadmill.

**De-risk** by restructuring: broad educational content that currently sits in the satisfiability middle ground. Add proprietary data. Add methodology layers an AI cannot synthesize from existing sources. The question isn't whether to abandon it — it's whether the content can be restructured from Tier 2 toward Tier 3. Per-page inspection (not per-cluster) produces more accurate results than broad category decisions.

**Double down** on: definitional and conceptual content covering foundational knowledge at genuine depth; proprietary methodology and original research; free tools and interactive calculators that require engagement to use; entity-rich conceptual frameworks where the engagement requirement is driven by complexity and expertise, not by the current limits of AI capability. These are the content types that held up or grew across 18 months and five independent verticals. The evidence is unusually consistent.

63% of the 118 companies in our study failed to grow organic traffic over the 18-month window. For most of them, the problem wasn't execution quality or topic selection. They had content on relevant topics. They published consistently. The underlying content was built to capture one-time queries. One-time queries are exactly what AI systems are now absorbing.

The topic didn't expire. The demand structure did.

---

Evergreen content wasn't wrong. It was incomplete.

For years, topic relevance was a sufficient filter because search engines were retrieval systems. They matched queries to content. AI changed the economics of retrieval. A system that can generate a competitor analysis template on demand doesn't need to send users to a page that offers one. The demand is still there. The mechanism for satisfying it changed.

The competitive advantage is no longer publishing content on topics that stay relevant. It's publishing content whose value survives even after the answer exists somewhere else. That's what durable demand measures. Not topics that last. Demand that renews — because the problem keeps recurring, and the audience keeps arriving.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Durable Demand Framework?

A five-step decision methodology for building and evaluating content that holds traffic as AI systems take on more of what search engines used to do. The five steps: (1) diagnose the mechanism driving traffic behavior, (2) build engagement-requiring content assets, (3) diversify across durable content types, (4) measure portfolio health, (5) apply the two-question test to every investment. The full framework is developed in the [Modern Organic Growth Playbook](/playbooks/modern-organic-growth-playbook).

### What is durable demand?

Demand that renews itself because the problem keeps recurring and the audience keeps arriving. Definitional content has durable demand because every year, a new cohort of practitioners needs to understand foundational concepts for the first time. Template content doesn't — the query resolves in a single transaction, and AI systems can now handle that transaction inside the SERP.

### What is engagement-requiring content?

Content whose value is only accessible through continued engagement. The reader must read, think, and apply — not extract and move on. A guide to how a DevOps pipeline actually works, a framework for building a compliance program, a methodology for approaching a strategic decision: these deliver their value through the reading itself. An AI system can summarize them. It cannot substitute for them.

### What is satisfaction-enabling content?

Content whose value can be fully extracted in a single interaction — a template, a list, a step-by-step process for a well-documented task. The user accomplishes their goal without engaging with the content at depth. That's exactly what AI systems can now provide inside the SERP, which is why this content type has seen severe traffic declines across the research cohort.

### Why isn't evergreen content enough?

Because evergreen content is a filter for topic longevity, not demand structure. A topic can remain permanently relevant while the demand underneath it collapses. Competitor analysis templates are an evergreen topic. Between 2025 and 2026, they lost 88% of their traffic — not because companies stopped needing competitor analysis, but because AI systems could deliver the artifact without a click. Evergreen tells you the topic will keep being searched. It tells you nothing about whether the content will keep earning the click.

### How do you know if content has durable demand?

Apply the two-question test: (1) Does this create ongoing engagement — does the reader need to read, think, and apply? (2) Can an AI system fully satisfy the query that would bring someone here without a click? If the answer to question 1 is yes and question 2 is no, the content has durable demand. If an AI Overview can fully accomplish the user's goal, the content is structurally vulnerable regardless of ranking position or execution quality.
