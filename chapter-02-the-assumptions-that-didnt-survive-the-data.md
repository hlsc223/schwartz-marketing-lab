# Chapter 2: The Assumptions That Didn't Survive the Data

---

The conventional wisdom about B2B organic growth in 2025 was confident, broadly shared, and frequently wrong.

Not wrong in every direction. The fundamentals of technical SEO, content quality, and authority building still mattered. But several of the assumptions most commonly used to justify content investment decisions — assumptions about what AI Overviews do, what traffic growth means, where growth comes from, and what produces compounding — did not survive contact with the data.

This chapter documents the four assumptions the cohort evidence most directly overturned. Each one represents a specific place where the conventional advice would have produced the wrong investment decision.

The goal isn't to establish that the industry was naive. It's to clear the priors that would cause a reader to misread what the rest of this report shows.

---

## Where the data pushed back

> **The assumptions most worth examining before investing are the ones that feel most self-evident. In this study, the beliefs held most confidently by the industry were the ones most consistently contradicted by the evidence.**

Four assumptions appeared repeatedly in how companies justified their content investments in this period. All four were challenged by what the cohort data showed. The challenges weren't marginal. They were directional.

## Assumption 1 — AI Overviews suppress informational content

**What the industry believed** AI Overviews would systematically reduce traffic to informational and educational content — the "what is X" and "how does X work" content that most B2B programs depend on. If AI can summarize any informational query, the concern went, the entire informational content category would lose CTR.

**What the data showed** Five companies — Rippling, Navan, Harness, Vanta, and Ramp — independently showed definitional content growing throughout the AI Overview expansion window. These companies operated in different categories: HR, Finance, DevOps, Security, FinTech. They used different content approaches. They grew at different rates. But all five showed the same pattern: foundational educational content was not broadly suppressed by AI Overviews.

The suppression that did occur followed a different pattern. Content whose primary value could be extracted and delivered in an Overview response — role lists, templates, brief definitions, step-by-step processes for well-documented tasks — experienced CTR collapse. Content that required continued engagement was largely intact.

The industry assumption produced a specific investment error: pulling back from the exact content type the data shows was most resilient. The suppression is query-specific, not category-wide. A program that treated all informational content as at risk abandoned a strong position based on a distinction the data didn't support.

---

## Assumption 2 — Traffic growth reflects program health

**What the industry believed** A growing organic traffic number means the program is working. Traffic is a reliable proxy for the health and effectiveness of content investment. More is better.

**What the data showed** Traffic and program health decoupled in multiple cases across the study.

LogRocket's organic traffic was 66% below its all-time peak while annual recurring revenue grew 22% year-over-year. The content program had contracted substantially; the business was growing through other channels. A team reading the organic number as a business health signal would have drawn the wrong conclusion in both directions: the business was healthier than the traffic suggested, and the content program was weaker than the revenue suggested. Chapter 3 establishes the decompositions that make these divergences visible in the data.

ZoomInfo's traffic grew 154.7% over the study window while traffic value grew only 14.7% over the same period — a 140-point divergence that Chapter 3 examines in detail. For now, the directional point is what matters: the composition of ZoomInfo's traffic shifted dramatically toward low-intent, low-value queries. The headline showed one of the strongest growth numbers in the cohort. The underlying composition told a fundamentally different story.

The business and the organic program can move in opposite directions. Traffic volume and traffic quality can move in opposite directions. None of those divergences is visible in the headline number alone. Before diagnosing any organic program, that number has to be decomposed into what is actually driving it.

---

## Assumption 3 — Concentrated bets drive growth

**What the industry believed** Organic growth comes from breakout assets — a single page, a cluster, or a campaign that achieves outsized rankings on high-volume queries. The strategic goal is to identify and scale that hero asset.

**What the data showed** In the growing companies with the cleanest organic signals during the study window, no single page exceeded approximately 20% of gross gains. Braze, Navan, Harness, Vanta, and Ramp all showed broad, distributed growth — dozens of pages each contributing modest individual amounts. The pattern was consistent across different categories, company sizes, and content approaches. Chapter 5 examines why this distribution appeared consistently across the winning programs and what drove it.

Programs concentrated in a hero asset showed a different risk profile. Concentration turns a single ranking change, algorithm update, or AI Overview interception into a program-level event. Distribution means any individual loss is absorbed by the breadth of the portfolio.

The counterintuitive implication: the most durable B2B content programs in this study looked unimpressive at the page level — because gains were spread thinly across a large number of pages. They only looked significant in aggregate. Programs chasing a hero asset were optimizing for a growth pattern the evidence doesn't support.

---

## Assumption 4 — Publishing more compounds growth

**What the industry believed** Content is a compounding asset. More pages create more entry points, more rankings, more traffic. The way to grow an organic program is to produce more.

**What the data showed** At scale, volume produced diminishing returns — and in several cases, negative returns.

LogRocket's cannibalization rate (gross loss as a percentage of gross gain) was 75.6%: for every unit of traffic the program gained, it lost 0.76 elsewhere in the portfolio. Aha!'s was 286.6%: every unit of traffic created was accompanied by 2.86 units of traffic lost. Both programs had been producing content at significant volume. The output of that production increasingly went toward replacing existing losses rather than building net-new traffic. Chapter 6 examines what healthy and unhealthy cannibalization rates look like across the full cohort.

At high cannibalization rates, publishing more content doesn't compound the program — it accelerates the treadmill. The production model of growth depends on a low cannibalization rate. Without it, volume makes the underlying problem worse while the headline may still show net-positive growth.

The implication isn't that content production is the wrong strategy. It's that production without portfolio management isn't a compounding strategy — it's a replacement strategy wearing a compounding strategy's name.

---

## Boundary conditions

**GitHub (scale doesn't protect):** GitHub's traffic decline is the clearest counterexample to the assumption that domain authority and scale provide structural protection. GitHub is among the most authoritative domains in developer search — decades of accumulated link equity, massive content breadth, institutional trust. It still declined substantially during the study window. Scale and authority create advantages in organic search; they don't create immunity. GitHub likely faced compound mechanisms — AI Overview interception on satisfiable developer queries, demand substitution as AI coding assistants matured — that no amount of historical authority could fully offset. The lesson is specific: authority is necessary but not sufficient when the mechanism driving a decline is structural.

> **Research synthesis**
>
> *Four assumptions commonly used to justify B2B content investment decisions in 2025 were each challenged or directly contradicted by cohort evidence: that AI Overviews suppress informational content broadly; that traffic growth reflects program health; that concentrated bets drive growth; and that publishing more compounds. In each case, the cohort data pointed in a different direction — not universally, but consistently enough to warrant treating each assumption as a prior to be examined rather than a foundation to build on.*

## Before you move on

Before advancing to the diagnostic chapters, run your current content roadmap against each of the four overturned assumptions. A roadmap built on any of these priors will systematically produce the wrong investment decisions.

| Assumption to examine | What to check on your roadmap |
|---|---|
| AI suppresses informational content broadly | Are you deprioritizing definitional or educational content due to AIO concern? Check whether those pages are satisfiable in a SERP response or require engagement. The suppression is query-specific. |
| Traffic growth = program health | Is the headline traffic number the primary metric driving content decisions? Run the three decompositions in Chapter 3 before drawing any conclusions from it. |
| Concentrated bets drive growth | Does your roadmap concentrate investment in building or protecting a single breakout asset? Calculate single-page concentration — if your top page accounts for more than 20% of gross gains, the program has concentration risk. |
| Publishing more compounds | Do you know your cannibalization rate? If not, hold that question until Chapter 6, which walks through the full calculation — run it before expanding production. A high rate means additional volume is feeding the treadmill, not building compounding authority. |
