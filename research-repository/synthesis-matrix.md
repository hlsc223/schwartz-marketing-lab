## Emerging patterns (2 supporting cases)

| Finding | Supporting companies | Confidence | Replicability | Client applicability | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| An acquisition can be measurably traced in organic search data via keyword-level demand transfer, accelerated by a fast, well-linked dedicated explainer/integration page | Gainsight (Skilljar, large-scale effect), Braze (OfferFit, well-corroborated but much smaller-scale effect — well under 2.5% of Braze's net traffic gain vs. Skilljar's larger apparent contribution at Gainsight) | High on the mechanism itself (dated, multi-touchpoint corroboration in both cases); note the *scale* of the effect varies substantially between cases, and now also appears to depend on *timing* relative to the acquisition (see Harness boundary-condition note in Hypotheses below) | High — any client with a recent acquisition can run this exact check | A concrete, low-cost playbook step for clients with M&A activity, now corroborated twice, with a timing caveat added via Harness's negative case. Caveat client expectations: the effect is real and traceable but its size depends heavily on the acquired brand's pre-existing search demand and how soon after the deal the data is measured — not every acquisition will move the topline number the way Skilljar did, and the effect appears to decay | **Emerging — watch for a third positive case to promote to Validated, and to clarify what drives the size and decay of the effect** (Navan was not an applicable case — no M&A activity in its window; Harness had a real acquisition but it predated the window by ~7 months and showed almost no measurable effect — see Hypotheses below) |
| A ranking breakthrough on an existing page, with little or no new backlink growth behind it, can drive a large share of a site's total traffic gain | Akita (two blog posts, zero backlinks on either, ~73% of net keyword-level gain), CloudBees (one git-tutorial blog post, backlinks predating the window by years, ~35% of net Top Pages gain) | High on the mechanism (clean, page-isolated, directly observed in both cases via zero or pre-existing backlink counts); the underlying trigger for the ranking jump itself could not be confirmed in either case (Ahrefs tracks rankings/links, not content-edit history) | High — any client can run the same page-level traffic-gain-vs-backlink-history check | A useful counter to "growth must mean either new content or new links": existing pages can break through to much higher rankings with no detectable new authority signal, making ranking/on-page audits of existing high-potential pages a candidate tactic distinct from publishing volume or link-building | **Emerging — two independent cases (Akita, CloudBees); watch for a third to promote to Validated, and for any case where the underlying trigger (content edit vs. algorithmic re-ranking) can be more directly confirmed** |

| Where content losses are concentrated determines whether a traffic decline is recoverable: companies whose algorithm-update losses fell on peripheral/topical content (not their core service-area foundational pages) recovered; companies whose losses fell on foundational pillar pages did not | **Hyperproof** (2026-06-30 — losses concentrated in incident response, FedRAMP, Executive Orders, DevSecOps — peripheral topical content; core GRC/risk framework pages held and grew → recovered to new all-time high); **Secureframe** (comparison brief, losses concentrated in what-is-soc-2, what-is-iso-27001, what-is-hipaa — core service-area foundational pages → did not recover, -35.2%); **Sprinto** (comparison brief, same mechanism as Secureframe, same category, same window — core foundational pages collapsed → did not recover, -39.2%) | High within these three cases — all in the same category, same window, directly comparable content audit against the same content-type taxonomy; the contrast is stark and internally consistent | High — directly auditable by any client: classify losing pages as "core service-area foundational content" vs. "topical/peripheral adjacent content" using a Top Pages export and one hour of review | Any client experiencing a traffic decline should immediately audit WHERE their losses are concentrated before deciding on a recovery strategy. Losses on your "what is X" and "what is [your primary service]" pages are more serious than losses on trending/topical content. This is the clearest single diagnostic in the study for triage prioritization | **Emerging — three supporting cases, all in Security/Compliance; strong internal evidence but needs at least one cross-category corroboration before Validated promotion** |
| A large developer-tutorial blog can sustain near-flat net traffic despite massive gross gains and gross losses (75.6% cannibalization rate), with the headline net growth driven primarily by branded demand rather than content improvement | **LogRocket** (2026-06-30 — 6,011 blog pages, gross gain +79,887 offset by gross loss -60,388, net blog traffic +2,624 (+2.9%); 3,150 pages dropped to zero traffic; headline +19.85% growth driven by homepage/branded traffic +163%, not by blog content); **Userpilot** (comparison brief — high gross losses -81.2%, content format directly vulnerable to algorithm-update cadence); **Cognism** (comparison brief — similar content-format vulnerability, milder severity -52.2%) | Medium-High — LogRocket is the primary case for the cannibalization-rate quantification; Userpilot and Cognism are supporting evidence for the content-format decay mechanism at lighter evidential weight | High — the gross gain/loss ratio (cannibalization rate) is directly calculable from any Top Pages export | A content-portfolio metric clients should track explicitly: if gross traffic losses are offsetting >50% of gross gains, the content program requires more new content investment just to stay even than its net figures suggest. A 75.6% cannibalization rate means the team must produce 4x the desired net gain in gross gains. This reframes the ROI conversation for large developer/informational content blogs | **Emerging — LogRocket is the primary quantified case; Userpilot and Cognism add directional support but at comparison-brief weight; watch for a second directly comparable gross gain/loss calculation** |

**The August 2025 spam update (Aug 26–Sep 22, 2025) is the new, distinct touchpoint this pass surfaces.** CloudBees's Phase 1 collapse and Ramp's collapse both align tightly to this same update, both show the same multi-bucket signature, and — critically — both are harder to explain away with a competing cause than the Dec 2025 core-update cases were: CloudBees's competing explanation (Sep 2025 layoffs) is explicitly rated weaker than the algorithm-update explanation for that specific inflection, and Ramp's competing explanation (its funding cadence) is directly contradicted by exact non-branded-traffic data for the same period. **Conclusion (updated):** algorithm updates, and spam updates specifically, now have their strongest evidentiary support in this study to date — still not a primary driver of any company's *headline* traffic classification, but a genuine, recurring, and increasingly well-isolated secondary mechanism affecting long-tail keyword footprint, observed independently in CloudBees and Ramp with the same update and the same signature. This is a meaningful upgrade from the original 11-company pass's "limited, non-dominant support" conclusion and should be flagged as the single highest-confidence algorithm-related finding to date for any client-facing write-up: a "Grew" headline classification does not rule out a real, spam-update-aligned long-tail collapse happening underneath it.

Actionable-output content (Aha! — role lists, templates, brief definitions) can be fully delivered in the SERP itself and WAS suppressed, with same-position CTR collapses of 79–99% directly observed across 20+ queries.

**Conclusion (updated):** AI Overview expansion is bidirectional in this cohort — five cases confirm resistance, one confirms suppression at the highest confidence yet. The differentiating variable is not content format alone but **query satisfiability**: can the AI Overview fully complete the user's task without a click? If yes, suppression is severe (Aha!); if no, the page can hold rankings and traffic (Rippling, Navan, Harness, Vanta, Ramp). This is the study's most actionable AI Overview finding and should be the primary framework for how clients evaluate their own exposure: classify existing content not by "informational vs. commercial" but by whether the AI Overview CAN provide the complete deliverable in the SERP.

### RQ3 — Where do meaningful mismatches exist between historical organic-traffic growth and current AI citation behavior, and what do those mismatches look like?

RQ3 is where the Scrunch sub-study is most informative. The four patterns below describe the recurring structural divergences between what drove organic traffic growth in the Phase 3 analysis and what AI engines currently retrieve and cite for equivalent queries.

**Pattern 1 — Historical content-led growth pages remain AI-visible when query intent and content type align** (Level 2: 7 of 9 confirmed citations at exact target URL)

The companies whose growth-asset pages were cited by AI engines share a common characteristic: the page type directly matches what AI systems appear to return for that query intent. Blog/Educational Articles are cited for how-to and educational queries (5 of 9 cases). Glossary Pages are cited for definitional queries (2 of 9 cases). An Interactive Tool is cited for a computation query (1 of 9 cases). In 7 of those 9 cases, the specific URL confirmed in Phase 3 as a historical growth driver is the exact URL the AI system returned — not a different page on the same domain. This is the study's clearest positive finding on RQ1/RQ3 overlap: for companies that grew through informational or definitional content investment, that content has remained retrievable by AI systems running comparable queries.

The pattern holds across 7 companies spanning different categories and content types (CircleCI in DevOps, Harness in DevOps, CloudBees in DevTools, Braze in Marketing Automation, Rippling in HR, Navan in Finance, PostHog in Product Analytics), which prevents it from being a category-specific artifact.

**Pattern 2 — Named-comparison prompts yield stronger cohort-brand citation than category prompts for the same topic** (Level 2: 5 of 6 companies with both prompt types show this direction)

When a cohort company is named as a participant in a comparison query ("How does X compare to Y?"), it appears in citation results on more engines than it does for category queries ("What is X software?") covering the same topic. In 5 of 6 companies tested across both prompt types, the comparison prompt produced more engine citations for that company:

- Rippling: 5 of 6 engines on "Rippling vs. Paylocity"; 0 of 6 on "What is an HRIS system?"
- PostHog: 4 of 6 engines on "PostHog vs. Mixpanel"; 0 of 6 on "What is product analytics software?"
- Braze: 1 of 6 engines on "Braze vs. ActiveCampaign"; 0 of 6 on "What is marketing automation software?"
- Vanta: 2 of 6 engines on "Vanta vs. Drata and Secureframe"; 2 of 6 on "What is SOC 2 compliance?" but with Drata and Sprinto taking those 2 slots, not Vanta's own pages leading them.
- CircleCI: 4 of 6 engines on "GitHub Actions vs CircleCI"; 0 of 6 on "What is a CI/CD pipeline?"

ZoomInfo is the partial exception: 2 of 6 engines on the category query "What is sales intelligence software?" (via pipeline.zoominfo.com) versus 1 of 6 on the comparison prompt "ZoomInfo vs. Cognism." ZoomInfo's content hub appears to have broader definitional authority than comparison-intent presence specifically.

The implication is that a company's AI citation presence on generic category queries is a structurally different and harder problem than its presence on branded comparison queries. Category queries route to established authorities by default; comparison queries require surfacing the named brands. A company invisible in category citation results may still be well-cited on comparison prompts — and vice versa.

**Pattern 3 — Commercial AI visibility reflects category authority more than historical traffic performance** (Level 2: visible across all 14 companies' commercial prompts)

Organic-traffic growth direction, as classified in Phase 3, does not predict whether a company appears in AI citation results for commercial queries ("What's the best X?"). ZoomInfo grew +154.7% in the Phase 3 window and is absent from commercial citation results on 0 of 6 engines for "What are the best B2B sales intelligence platforms?" Braze grew +87.4% and returned 0 of 6 citations for "What are the best marketing automation platforms for mobile apps?" PostHog grew +131.4% and returned 0 of 6 citations for "What are the best product analytics tools for engineering teams?" Conversely, Vanta appeared on 4 of 6 engines for "What's the best SOC 2 compliance automation software?" and Harness appeared on 2 of 6 engines for "What's the best enterprise CI/CD platform?" — both also strong growers, but in categories where their brand authority with AI systems appears to be more established.

The commercial citation results for cohort companies that do appear are generally dominated by third-party review aggregators (G2, Capterra, TrustRadius, Forbes, PCMag, Reddit) that hold the category citation space ahead of vendor pages. Cohort companies appear within those results only when the company's brand-level recognition in the category is strong enough to break into AI systems' retrieved sources alongside the aggregators. Raw traffic growth alone does not establish this.

**Pattern 4 — google_ai_mode and google_ai_overviews are the primary citation surfaces for cohort-company content; chatgpt, copilot, and google_gemini are substantially less likely to cite cohort pages** (Level 2: observed across all 14 companies and all prompt categories)

Across all 49 prompts and all prompt types, google_ai_mode and google_ai_overviews were the engines most consistently returning cohort-company pages in citation results. chatgpt cited cohort companies on commercial and comparison prompts (Vanta on SOC 2 commercial, Freshworks on best help desk, Gainsight on best customer success platforms, Rippling on best all-in-one HR, Ramp and Rippling on the startup card commercial prompt) but rarely surfaced cohort companies on category or growth-asset prompts where query intent is educational or definitional. copilot's results were populated by low-authority review/comparison blogs in a number of cases rather than cohort pages or established authorities. google_gemini returned image thumbnail URLs (encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com) as its citation results for multiple category and commercial prompts — these are not content citations and were excluded from analysis; meaningful Gemini citations were concentrated on comparison and some educational prompts. perplexity was strongest on named-comparison prompts (PostHog vs. Mixpanel, CircleCI deployment strategies) but largely absent from category and commercial prompts where cohort companies were also absent from other engines.

For this cohort, AI discoverability is currently driven primarily by the Google ecosystem. A company cited on google_ai_mode and google_ai_overviews but not on chatgpt, copilot, or perplexity is visible in Google's AI products and largely absent from the others — these are not interchangeable citation surfaces.

**Level 1 Finding — Operational-status queries route exclusively to specialist third-party tools; product pages are not cited regardless of relevance** (single prompt, all 6 engines)

All 42 citations across 6 engines for "How do I check if a website is down right now?" went to dedicated uptime-monitoring tools: downdetector.com, isitdownrightnow.com, eb-alert.io, ohdear.app, onlineornot.com, and similar services. Freshworks's `/website-monitoring/is-it-down/` was not cited on any engine. This is a query-routing finding rather than a company-specific failure: AI systems appear to classify operational-status queries as real-time factual lookups and return tools built for that purpose, not product or feature pages that describe monitoring capabilities. The consistency across all 6 engines is notable — this is the one prompt in the dataset where the same routing behavior appeared on every engine without exception.

**Level 1 Finding — A company's largest organic-traffic-gaining page and its AI-cited content footprint can operate in entirely different topic areas** (single company: Vanta)

Vanta's `/collection/grc/risk-management-frameworks` (+480→21,462 traffic, ~45% of Vanta's net Top Pages gain in Phase 3) was not cited on any engine for "What is a risk management framework?" — a query where NIST, AWS, Cybersaint, Diligent, and MetricStream displaced all vendor content. Vanta IS cited on google_ai_mode and google_ai_overviews for "What is SOC 2 compliance?" and on chatgpt, google_ai_mode, google_ai_overviews, and google_gemini for "What's the best SOC 2 compliance automation software?" — 4 of 6 engines on the commercial query, the cohort's strongest commercial citation result. Two distinct AI citation profiles operate simultaneously: the broad GRC/risk-framework topic area, where Vanta's traffic-driving content is absent from AI retrieval, and the SOC 2/compliance-automation product category, where Vanta competes strongly. This is informative for one company at this stage, not a confirmed cross-company pattern.

**Level 1 Finding — CloudBees is not cited for its primary commercial query despite owning the Jenkins ecosystem** (single company, single prompt)

"What's the best Jenkins alternative for CI/CD?" returned 0 CloudBees citations across 6 engines. Harness was cited on google_ai_overviews and google_gemini. This is consistent with the Phase 3 deep dive's hypothesis that CloudBees's commercial/Jenkins-specific pages lost rankings while its generic git-tutorial content grew — the citation data adds a current cross-section to that historical trajectory and suggests the commercial visibility gap is reflected in AI citation behavior, not only in organic rankings.

**Level 3 Research Implications**

The growth-asset citation results (Pattern 1) confirm that historically high-performing informational and definitional content pages can be retrieved by AI systems for equivalent queries — but this holds under specific conditions: query intent is educational or definitional, content type directly matches that intent, and the company has sufficient category recognition to appear alongside established authorities. When those conditions are not met — as with the Freshworks operational-routing finding, the Vanta institutional-authority displacement finding, and the Gainsight specialist-tool competition finding — historical organic-growth performance offers no reliable prediction of AI citation presence.

The commercial and category citation findings (Pattern 3) surface the study's primary divergence: organic-search presence and AI-citation presence are not the same thing, and the gap is concentrated in the query types most relevant to demand generation. Companies invisible in AI citation results for category and commercial queries may still hold strong organic rankings for those same topics — the two systems have different citation logics, different source hierarchies, and different query-routing behaviors. This divergence is not uniformly distributed across companies or content types; it is sharpest for companies in categories where third-party aggregators have established authority with AI systems, and weakest for companies with either named-brand comparison presence or long-form how-to/definitional content that directly matches a query's informational intent.

### Concluding synthesis — what this sub-study contributes to the overall research

The Scrunch citation study was designed to complement the Phase 3 Ahrefs analysis, not replace it. The two instruments measure different things: Ahrefs documents historical organic-search behavior over a defined window; Scrunch captures a cross-sectional snapshot of AI retrieval behavior at a single point in time for equivalent user queries. Together, they allow a question the Ahrefs analysis alone cannot answer — whether the content patterns that drove organic-search growth in the study window have translated into AI-era discoverability.

The answer this dataset supports is: sometimes, and for specific reasons. In 7 of 13 growth-asset cases, the exact URL identified in Phase 3 as a historical traffic driver was also returned by AI engines for the equivalent query. The content types most frequently cited by AI systems — Blog/Educational Articles and Glossary Pages — are the same types that drove the majority of cohort traffic growth in the Phase 3 analysis. That alignment holds across companies in different categories, different sizes, and different growth mechanisms, which prevents it from being a category-specific artifact. Where the two instruments diverge, the divergence is not random: non-citation occurred consistently when query intent routed to specialist tools, when topic authority was held by institutional sources that displace vendor content regardless of content quality, or when the queried company lacked sufficient brand-level recognition in the AI system's retrieval logic for that specific topic. Commercial and category queries show the sharpest divergence — AI systems in these contexts route to third-party aggregators and established authorities, and organic-traffic growth direction does not reliably predict whether a company appears in those results.

What this sub-study contributes, then, is not a verdict on whether AI is "replacing" organic search, but a more precise picture of where the two systems agree and where they operate on different logics. AI retrieval behavior depends on query intent, content type, competing authority sources, and engine-specific citation patterns in ways that are partly independent of historical SEO performance. A company's discoverability in the AI era is a genuinely two-dimensional problem — and the conditions under which search-era success transfers to AI-era visibility are conditional, not universal.
