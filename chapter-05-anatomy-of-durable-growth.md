# Chapter 5: The Anatomy of Durable Growth

---

The prevailing model for B2B content strategy assumes that growth is discoverable: that somewhere in the search landscape, in some topic, format, or angle, there is a move that will produce disproportionate results if identified and executed well enough. The strategist's job, under this model, is to find that move and scale it.

This assumption shapes how most content programs are built. It explains the obsession with pillar pages: the single authoritative asset at the center of a topic cluster. And it explains the expectation that a high-performing organic program traces back to one piece doing most of the work.

The data from this study doesn't support it.

Across the content-driven growing companies in this study, no single page or content cluster was responsible for more than roughly 20% of gross gains. Growth didn't come from a hero asset. It came from a broad portfolio: dozens of pages distributed across a category, anchored in a type of content the industry had recently begun to write off.

Understanding what drove that growth means looking at three things: the distribution pattern, the content type that made it possible, and the position dynamics that signal whether a program is building on durable or fragile ground.

---

## The distribution pattern

*[Figure 5.1 — Content distribution chart. See visual notes at end of chapter.]*

Start with Braze. Across the study window, Braze grew organic traffic consistently while most of its peers in marketing automation declined. When you look at the pages driving that growth, no single page stands out. The largest contributor accounted for roughly 15–18% of gross gains. The next several pages contributed between 8–12% each. Below that, dozens of pages each contributed smaller amounts: none individually remarkable, but collectively substantial.

This is not what most people expect to find in a high-performing content program. The expectation is a clear peak: one or two pages accounting for a majority of traffic, with a long tail of supporting pages contributing smaller amounts. What Braze showed was closer to an even distribution: many pages contributing meaningfully, no dramatic outliers.

The pattern appeared again in Harness. And in Navan. And in Ramp. And in Vanta. Across different categories, different program structures, different company sizes. Same distribution, same result.

> **Research synthesis**
>
> *This was not a Braze or marketing automation story. The same distribution appeared across DevOps, finance, travel, and security: a cohort pattern, not an execution anomaly.*

---

## Why definitional content behaves differently

The distribution exists because of the content type behind it. Across the winning cohort, the pages generating consistent gains were definitional: "What is customer engagement." "What is continuous integration." "What is accounts payable." "What is SOC 2." Pages that answer a question about a concept, not a product or tactic, and answer it well enough that the reader leaves with a working understanding of the thing.

### The demand renews itself

What makes this content category durable is not primarily that the definitions are stable, though they often are. The explanation is enduring learning demand: demand driven not by news cycles, product launches, or trending topics, but by the continuous arrival of new people into professional roles where these concepts matter.

Every year, new engineers need to understand what continuous integration means in practice. Every year, companies begin the SOC 2 process for the first time. Every year, finance teams at growing companies encounter accounts payable processes they haven't managed before. The specific tools change constantly. The foundational concepts don't. The people who need to learn them keep arriving.

The evidence in this study is consistent with this explanation. The same content type held up across five independent categories during a period when AI tools were absorbing routine tasks and demand for tactical how-to content was contracting:

- Rippling: HR and people operations
- Navan and Ramp: finance and spend management
- Harness: DevOps
- Vanta: security and compliance

These companies serve different buyers and operate in different competitive environments. The durability appears to be a property of the content, not a category anomaly.

### The boundary condition: Aha!

There is an important limit to this pattern, and Aha! helps define it.

Aha! had a substantial, well-developed content program and still declined significantly. Its content wasn't definitional in the sense described above. It was strategic: frameworks for product roadmapping, methodologies for team prioritization, thought leadership on product strategy. Informational, certainly, but often summarizable without requiring deeper engagement.

### The real test: satisfiability

> **The relevant question is not "is this informational content?" The relevant question is: can the user accomplish their goal from an AI Overview or chat response alone, without clicking through? If the answer is yes, the content is structurally at risk — regardless of how well-executed it is. This is the question Chapter 11 formalizes as Question 2 of the two-question investment test.**

Aha!'s experience makes this distinction visible in a way that five growing companies can't. Growth confirms that a content type works; Aha! clarifies why — by showing what happens when content looks similar on the surface but lacks the property that makes it resistant to interception.

> **Research synthesis**
>
> *The durability of definitional content is not about format or length. It depends on whether the content requires engagement to deliver value. If a reader can skim a summary and move on, the content is at risk.*

## Position consolidation as a diagnostic metric

The third pattern in the growing cohort is more counterintuitive than the first two, and it's one that most content programs would misread as a warning sign.

In the majority of growing companies examined, positions 21–50 and 51+, the long tail of lower-ranking keywords, shrank during the study window. At the same time, positions 1–3 and 4–10 grew or held. The top of the position distribution strengthened. The bottom thinned out.

The standard interpretation of a contracting long tail is concern. Fewer ranking keywords means less potential traffic, less topical coverage, a smaller footprint. If the objective is to rank for as many keywords as possible, a shrinking 21–50 bucket looks like a problem.

The data points to a different interpretation, and a different metric.

In the context of these programs, the contraction in the long tail happened alongside strengthening in top positions, and the net effect was growth, not decline. Low-quality rankings at positions 40–60 disappeared while the program's strongest pages moved deeper into positions where traffic actually flows.

| | Old metric | Better metric |
|---|---|---|
| What to track | Total ranking keywords | Share of rankings in positions 1–10 |
| What growth looks like | More keywords at any position | Top positions growing, long tail consolidating |
| What the risk looks like | Keywords disappearing | Positions 21+ expanding while 1–10 stays flat |

> **A shrinking long tail is only a warning sign if top positions are shrinking too.**

A program where the percentage of keywords in positions 1–10 is growing quarter over quarter is gaining ground. A program where positions 21+ are expanding as a share of total rankings is accumulating fragile exposure: the kind that doesn't survive algorithm updates or AI Overview expansion. The headline keyword count can look the same in both cases, which is exactly why it's the wrong thing to measure.

> **Research synthesis**
>
> *Across all three patterns (distributed gains, definitional content, position consolidation), the winning programs were building portfolios designed to absorb shocks. The distribution of gains meant no single point of failure. The content type meant structurally renewable demand: demand that replenishes as new practitioners continuously arrive. The position distribution meant top-position authority was the floor, not the ceiling.*

## The re-ranking exception

In at least two cases, traffic growth came not from new content but from existing pages ascending in the rankings, without any detectable new investment in backlinks or significant content updates.

Akita's breakthrough in customer success was driven primarily by a small number of existing pages moving from mid-tier positions into the top three or four results. CloudBees showed a similar pattern: a git-related tutorial page that had existed for some time moved from relative obscurity into high-visibility rankings, generating a meaningful portion of the company's traffic gains.

What drove these re-ranking events isn't fully attributable from the available data. In both cases, the pages that ascended were definitional or educational, consistent with the broader pattern. Whether the improvement came from changes in competing pages, accumulated engagement signals, or algorithm adjustments isn't clear.

The implication is clear: growth doesn't always require new content. A program that only builds new assets and never audits what it already has overlooks a legitimate path to growth. The best existing pages may be underperforming for reasons that have more to do with the competitive landscape than with the content itself.

---

## Boundary conditions

Two cases from the cohort don't fit the pattern described above, and they matter because they define what the pattern can and cannot claim.

**Boundary condition 1: brand demand (PostHog)**
PostHog grew +131% in organic traffic during the study window. It didn't do it with definitional content or a distributed educational program. It did it primarily with brand demand: people searching specifically for PostHog by name. As more developers used and recommended the product, more people searched for it directly, and organic traffic grew as a result. The mechanism is a product adoption story, not a content strategy story.

This is a real and substantial outcome. But it's not replicable through content investment, because the content wasn't what produced it. PostHog's experience confirms that organic growth is possible through multiple mechanisms, and that the distributed definitional content pattern is one of them, not the only one.

**Boundary condition 2: niche concentration (Akita)**
Akita showed a pattern closer to single-page concentration than any other clean-growth company in the cohort. A breakthrough on a small number of existing pages accounted for a significant share of its gains. This is the case that most directly challenges the "no hero asset" finding.

But even Akita's concentrated growth was driven by two pages, not one. And the breakthrough happened in customer success, a category where Akita competes as a smaller player against better-resourced alternatives. The re-ranking likely reflected a competitive gap in the landscape as much as anything Akita did directly. The lesson here is narrow: in underpenetrated niches where the competitive set is weak, a concentrated bet can break through. That's not a template for a program competing in a mature market.

**The broader cohort context**

A classification pass across all 44 growing companies reinforces what these two boundary conditions define. Among 38 full-weight classifiable growers, definitional and educational content was the most common primary growth pattern: 23 of 38 companies, or 61%.

More telling than the share is where each pattern appeared. DE was the only bucket consistently represented across all five trajectory types, including Growth then Pullback and Decline then Recovery, the two trajectories where resilience mattered more than momentum. Brand-led growth appeared in five Continuous Acceleration cases and one Growth then Pullback case, but in no Decline then Recovery cases. This sharpens the durability argument: DE programs appear where other mechanisms largely don't, though the classification pass alone does not explain why.

Neither PostHog nor Akita invalidates the pattern. They define its scope: the distributed definitional content model describes content-driven organic growth in competitive markets. Brand momentum and niche concentration are different mechanisms with different prerequisites.

---

## What one sentence should you remember from this chapter?

Durable growth, across the companies in this study that produced it through content, came from broad programs built around definitional content. Not from finding the one thing that worked and scaling it.

That's the pattern. It's not a hypothesis or a framework derived from first principles. It's what happened, across five categories, in conditions where most approaches were failing.

---

## Before you move on

Pull your Top Pages report and calculate what share of gross gains over the past 12 months came from your top-performing page. Among the cleanest growers in this study, no single page exceeded roughly 20% of gross gains. If your top page sits well above that, ask what happens if it loses its ranking.

Then identify two or three content areas adjacent to your strongest performers where you have little or no presence. Those are your first diversification targets.
